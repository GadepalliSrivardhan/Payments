13.1 Cryptocurrencies — Overview

Cryptocurrencies are digital assets that use cryptographic techniques to secure transactions and control the creation of new units. Unlike traditional currencies issued by central banks, most cryptocurrencies operate on decentralised networks with no central authority. Bitcoin, launched in January 2009 by the pseudonymous Satoshi Nakamoto, was the first cryptocurrency and introduced the concept of a peer-to-peer electronic cash system using a distributed ledger (blockchain).

₿

Bitcoin (BTC)

First cryptocurrency. Fixed supply of 21 million coins. Proof-of-Work consensus. ~10-minute block time. Primarily a store of value ("digital gold"). Settlement finality is probabilistic.

⟠

Ethereum (ETH)

Programmable blockchain supporting smart contracts and decentralised applications (dApps). Migrated to Proof-of-Stake in 2022. Enables DeFi, NFTs, and tokenised assets.

💲

Stablecoins

Cryptocurrencies pegged to a stable asset (USD, EUR, gold). Examples: USDT (Tether), USDC (Circle), DAI (algorithmic). Aim to combine crypto's programmability with fiat's stability.

🔗

Altcoins

Thousands of alternative cryptocurrencies: XRP (Ripple — cross-border payments), Stellar (XLM), Solana (SOL — high throughput), Litecoin (LTC), and many others with specific use cases.

Regulatory Landscape

Crypto regulation varies significantly by jurisdiction. The EU's MiCA (Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation), fully applicable from December 2024, provides comprehensive regulation for crypto-asset issuers and service providers across the EU. The US takes a multi-regulator approach (SEC, CFTC, FinCEN) with ongoing debate over which assets are securities. China has banned private cryptocurrency trading while developing the e-CNY CBDC. Most jurisdictions require crypto exchanges to comply with AML/KYC rules under FATF guidance.

13.1b Bitcoin as a Payment System — Limitations

Satoshi Nakamoto's 2008 whitepaper described Bitcoin as a "peer-to-peer electronic cash system" — implying a payment system free from intermediaries, accessible globally, and censorship-resistant. In practice, Bitcoin has largely failed to achieve this vision at scale. Understanding why is essential for evaluating DLT-based payment proposals.

Practical Failures as a Payment System

1

Scalability — ~7 TPS

Bitcoin's base layer processes approximately 7 transactions per second (TPS) — constrained by a 1 MB block size and ~10-minute block time. Compare this to Visa's 24,000+ TPS (peak capacity). Even Ethereum's base layer reaches only ~15–30 TPS. This throughput gap makes Bitcoin unusable as a global payment rail without Layer 2 solutions.

2

Confirmation Time — ~1 Hour for Finality

Each Bitcoin block takes approximately 10 minutes to mine. Probabilistic finality requires 6 confirmations (~1 hour) for high-value transactions, as earlier blocks can theoretically be reorganised. In practice, merchants accepting Bitcoin for point-of-sale must either wait an hour or accept the risk of a zero-confirmation transaction.

3

Transaction Fees — Spikes to $50+

Bitcoin transaction fees are determined by a market: users bid to have their transactions included in the next block. During periods of network congestion — notably in 2017 and 2021 — average fees spiked above $50 per transaction. This makes Bitcoin economically unviable for micropayments or everyday retail use, where the fee may exceed the transaction value.

4

Price Volatility — Cannot Denominate Prices

Bitcoin's exchange rate against fiat currencies can swing 50% or more within weeks. A merchant cannot price goods in BTC — by the time the invoice is paid, the real value has changed materially. Stablecoins were invented precisely to address this limitation, but they are not Bitcoin. Volatility also creates foreign exchange risk for recipients who hold BTC after receipt.

5

Energy Consumption — ~130 TWh Annually

Bitcoin's Proof-of-Work consensus mechanism requires miners to expend enormous computational energy. Estimated annual consumption is approximately 130 TWh — comparable to the entire electricity use of Argentina. This environmental cost is a significant barrier to institutional and governmental adoption, particularly as ESG considerations grow in importance.

Lightning Network — Layer 2 Solution

The Lightning Network is a Layer 2 protocol built on top of Bitcoin designed to enable fast, low-cost micropayments by moving most transactions off the main blockchain (off-chain). It addresses scalability and fee issues without changing Bitcoin's base layer.

Payment Channels

Two parties open a payment channel by locking Bitcoin in a multi-signature (multi-sig) on-chain contract. Once the channel is open, the two parties can transact with each other an unlimited number of times — exchanging signed balance updates off-chain — without broadcasting each transaction to the Bitcoin network. Only the opening and closing transactions are recorded on the blockchain. This dramatically reduces on-chain load and enables near-instant, near-zero-cost transactions between the two parties.

Routing Across the Network

Lightning's power extends beyond direct channels: payments can be routed through a network of payment channels. If Alice has a channel with Bob, and Bob has a channel with Carol, Alice can pay Carol by routing through Bob — using Hash Time-Locked Contracts (HTLCs) to ensure the payment is atomic (either fully succeeds or fully refunds). This enables a web of connected channels to serve as a payment network.

Lightning Network Limitations: Requires both parties to have online nodes to receive payments (no offline receipt). Channel liquidity must be actively managed — a channel cannot route more than its locked balance. Routing large payments can fail if no path with sufficient liquidity exists. Lightning is limited to Bitcoin and cannot natively bridge to other blockchains or fiat currencies. Despite these constraints, it is the most developed Layer 2 for BTC payments, with millions of active channels.

13.2 Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT)

A Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT) is a database that is shared, replicated, and synchronised across multiple nodes (computers) in a network without a central administrator. Unlike a traditional centralised database (run by a single bank or operator), a DLT's records are maintained by a network of participants, each holding a copy of the complete ledger.

Permissioned vs Permissionless DLT

Permissionless (Public): Anyone can join as a node, validate transactions, and read the ledger. High decentralisation, lower privacy, slower throughput. Examples: Bitcoin, Ethereum. Suitable for trustless environments.
Permissioned (Private/Consortium): Only pre-approved participants can join and validate. Higher privacy, faster throughput, better governance. Examples: Hyperledger Fabric, R3 Corda, Quorum. Preferred for institutional finance.

Core DLT Properties

  • Decentralisation: No single point of control or failure
  • Immutability: Once recorded, data cannot be altered without consensus of the network
  • Transparency: All participants can verify the ledger state (varies in permissioned systems)
  • Cryptographic Security: Transactions are signed and chained using cryptographic hashes
  • Consensus: All nodes agree on the valid state of the ledger via consensus protocols

Enterprise DLT Platforms for Payments

🔷

Hyperledger Fabric

Linux Foundation project. Modular, permissioned blockchain. Uses channels for data privacy between subsets of participants. Widely used in trade finance (we.trade, Contour) and supply chain.

🔴

R3 Corda

Designed specifically for financial institutions. Peer-to-peer architecture — data shared only between transaction parties (not broadcast to all nodes). Used by major banks for bond issuance, FX, and repo.

⚡

Quorum (ConsenSys)

Enterprise Ethereum fork. Supports private transactions. Used by JPMorgan for JPM Coin (interbank USD transfers) and the Onyx platform.

🌊

Ripple / XRP Ledger

Consensus protocol (not PoW/PoS). Purpose-built for cross-border payments. Fast settlement (3–5 seconds), low cost. Used by banks via RippleNet for on-demand liquidity.

13.3 Blockchain in Payments

Tokenisation

Tokenisation is the process of representing a real-world asset (currency, bond, equity, commodity, real estate) as a digital token on a blockchain. A tokenised dollar, for example, is a digital representation of a USD claim that can be transferred instantly on a blockchain. Tokenisation enables: fractional ownership, programmable money (with smart contracts), instant atomic settlement, and 24/7 trading.

Atomic Swaps

An atomic swap is a smart-contract-based mechanism that enables two parties to exchange different cryptocurrency tokens across different blockchains without a trusted intermediary. The swap is "atomic" — it either fully completes (both legs execute) or fully reverts (neither leg executes). This is the blockchain equivalent of PvP (Payment vs Payment) and DvP (Delivery vs Payment) — eliminating settlement risk without a CLS Bank or central counterparty.

Cross-Border Payment Use Cases

1

On-Demand Liquidity (ODL)

Ripple's ODL model: sender converts fiat to XRP, XRP is transferred across the ledger in seconds, receiver converts XRP to destination currency. Eliminates the need for pre-funded nostro accounts.

2

Stablecoin Remittance

USD stablecoins (USDC, USDT) are increasingly used for remittances. Sender converts fiat to stablecoin, transfers near-instantly on Ethereum/Stellar/Solana, receiver off-ramps to local currency. Lower cost than SWIFT for small amounts.

3

Trade Finance on DLT

Letters of credit and trade documents tokenised on Hyperledger Fabric. Parties (importer, exporter, banks, insurer, shipping) share a common view. Reduces processing from 5–10 days to hours.

Blockchain Settlement Finality: Public blockchains offer probabilistic finality — a transaction is "final" after enough confirmations (Bitcoin: ~6 blocks = ~1 hour). Permissioned DLTs like Corda and Fabric offer deterministic finality — a transaction is immediately final once consensus is reached. Deterministic finality is essential for financial settlement.

13.3b Tokenised Deposits vs CBDC

A critical distinction for practitioners: not all "digital money on a blockchain" is CBDC. Tokenised deposits are commercial bank liabilities represented as tokens on a DLT — they carry the same credit risk as a bank deposit. CBDCs are central bank liabilities — risk-free in the sense that the central bank cannot default in its own currency. This difference has profound implications for systemic risk and financial stability.

JPM Coin — Case Study

🏦

What is JPM Coin?

Launched in 2019, JPM Coin is a tokenised deposit running on J.P. Morgan's proprietary permissioned blockchain platform Onyx, built on Quorum (an enterprise Ethereum fork). Each JPM Coin represents a claim of one US dollar held at JP Morgan Bank — it is not a cryptocurrency and it is not a CBDC.

🔄

Use Cases

JPM Coin is used for: intraday USD transfers between institutional clients of JP Morgan; cross-border payments settling on the Onyx platform; and 24/7 settlement, giving clients access to dollar liquidity outside Fedwire's business hours (08:30–18:30 ET). This addresses the operational window gap in US dollar settlement.

⚠️

NOT a CBDC

JPM Coin is a commercial bank liability — it represents a deposit claim on JP Morgan, not on the Federal Reserve. If JP Morgan were to fail, JPM Coin holders would be unsecured creditors. CBDC holders, by contrast, hold claims directly on the central bank. This distinction matters for systemic risk analysis and regulatory treatment.

🌐

HSBC Orion

HSBC's Orion platform is a comparable initiative — a tokenised deposit and digital asset issuance platform used for tokenised bond issuance and settlement. Like JPM Coin, it operates on a permissioned DLT and represents a commercial bank liability, not central bank money.

Key Insight for Practitioners: The emergence of tokenised deposits by major commercial banks represents a pragmatic, near-term path to blockchain-based settlement — without waiting for central bank CBDC launches. However, they fragment liquidity across bank-specific platforms and do not eliminate the credit risk of commercial bank money. The long-term vision of the BIS and central banks is for wCBDC to provide a risk-free "settlement asset" at the core of tokenised financial markets.

13.4 Smart Contracts

A smart contract is a self-executing programme stored on a blockchain that automatically enforces and executes the terms of an agreement when pre-defined conditions are met — without the need for intermediaries. The concept was first described by Nick Szabo in 1994 and practically realised with Ethereum's launch in 2015. Smart contracts are written in programming languages like Solidity (Ethereum) or Kotlin/Java (Corda) and run on the blockchain's virtual machine.

📜

Trade Finance

Smart contract releases payment automatically when shipping documents are verified on-chain and delivery conditions confirmed. Replaces letter of credit manual processing.

📊

Derivatives Settlement

OTC derivative contracts coded as smart contracts on Ethereum. Margin calls calculated and executed automatically based on real-time price feeds. ISDA has published smart contract templates.

🔒

Escrow & Conditional Payments

Funds locked in a smart contract escrow, released when both parties confirm conditions are met (e.g., milestone payments in construction contracts, real estate settlement).

🏦

DeFi (Decentralised Finance)

Smart contracts power lending protocols (Aave, Compound), decentralised exchanges (Uniswap), and yield farming — financial services without traditional intermediaries.

Smart Contract Limitations

  • The Oracle Problem: Smart contracts can only access data already on the blockchain. For real-world data (prices, events, delivery confirmation), they need "oracles" — external data feeds (e.g., Chainlink). Oracles introduce a centralisation and trust dependency.
  • Code is Law — and Bugs: Smart contracts are immutable once deployed. Bugs in the code become permanent vulnerabilities. The 2016 "DAO hack" drained $60M from an Ethereum smart contract due to a reentrancy bug.
  • Legal Enforceability: Smart contract code execution and legal contract obligations may not align. Most jurisdictions are still developing frameworks for smart contract legal recognition.
  • Scalability: Complex smart contracts consume significant "gas" (computational cost) on public blockchains, making them expensive for high-frequency use cases.

13.4b Smart Contracts — Legal Framework

The legal status of smart contracts is one of the most important unresolved questions in financial law. While code can automatically execute a payment, whether that execution constitutes — or replaces — a legally binding contract under common law or civil law systems requires careful analysis.

UK Law Commission — Digital Assets 2023

The UK Law Commission's 2023 report on Digital Assets provided a landmark analysis. Its key finding: smart contracts can form legally binding contracts under English law, provided they meet the standard requirements for contract formation:

🤝

Offer & Acceptance

The interaction between parties deploying and invoking a smart contract can constitute offer and acceptance — but only if there is demonstrable mutual assent. Code execution alone may not establish this if the parties did not intend to create legal relations.

💰

Consideration

The exchange of tokens or digital assets can constitute consideration, satisfying this contract formation requirement. However, purely automated value transfers without clear party intent may raise questions about whether consideration flows in a legally meaningful sense.

🧠

Intention to Create Legal Relations

This is the most contested element. Deploying a smart contract on a public blockchain may not, by itself, demonstrate the parties intended to create legally enforceable obligations — particularly where the parties are pseudonymous.

⚙️

New Category of Property

The Law Commission also recommended recognising digital assets as a third category of personal property under English law (beyond "things in possession" and "things in action") — significant for establishing rights in crypto-assets and tokenised securities.

Key Legal Uncertainties

1

Jurisdiction

A smart contract deployed on a public, global blockchain has no domicile. Which country's courts have jurisdiction? Which country's law governs the contract? The answers are unclear, particularly for disputes between parties in different jurisdictions interacting with the same contract. Traditional conflict-of-laws rules were not designed for stateless, borderless code.

2

Immutability vs Contract Amendment

Contract law generally permits parties to mutually agree to amend or terminate an agreement. Blockchain immutability means a deployed smart contract cannot be modified — the only recourse is deploying a new contract (if the original allows upgrade logic) or migrating state. This tension between legal flexibility and technical permanence has no clean resolution.

3

Mistake and Frustration

Common law allows a contract to be unwound if it was entered under a fundamental mistake, or if subsequent events make performance impossible (frustration). A blockchain does not recognise these doctrines — if a smart contract executes erroneously, the transaction is permanent. This creates a gap between legal remedies and technical reality.

4

Consumer Protection

Automated, irreversible execution may prevent consumers from exercising statutory rights — such as the right to cancel, chargeback, or seek redress. Consumer protection law typically requires the ability to reverse transactions or pursue the contracting party; this is fundamentally at odds with blockchain finality.

ISDA Common Domain Model (CDM)

The International Swaps and Derivatives Association (ISDA) has developed the Common Domain Model (CDM) — an initiative to standardise how derivatives contracts are represented in machine-readable, executable code, enabling smart contract execution of over-the-counter (OTC) derivatives.

What CDM Does

  • Defines a common digital representation of trade events: new trade, amendment, termination, novation, reset, and settlement
  • Provides a standardised data model — the same fields, same logic, same terminology across all market participants
  • Published as open-source code (Rosetta DSL / Java/Python) so any firm can implement it
  • Endorsed and maintained by ISDA, ICMA, ISLA, and the CDM Steering Working Group

CDM in Smart Contracts

  • Smart contracts using CDM can auto-calculate and execute margin calls, periodic payments (fixed/floating coupons), and final settlements without manual intervention
  • Reduces operational risk from manual processing of complex derivatives — a major source of trade failures and reconciliation breaks
  • Enables atomic DvP settlement of derivatives: cash payment and securities delivery triggered simultaneously by the same smart contract
  • Being used in Project Guardian (MAS) and other tokenised derivatives pilots
CDM Significance: The derivatives market processes hundreds of trillions in notional value annually, largely through manual and semi-manual processes. If CDM enables widespread smart contract automation of OTC derivatives lifecycle events, the reduction in operational cost, settlement failures, and counterparty risk could be one of the largest efficiency gains in financial market history.

13.5 Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) — Fundamentals

A Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) is a digital form of central bank money — a liability of the central bank, denominated in the national currency, and available in digital form. CBDCs differ from existing electronic money (bank deposits) in that they are direct claims on the central bank rather than on a commercial bank. They also differ from cryptocurrencies in that they are centralised, regulated, and stable in value.

Retail vs Wholesale CBDC

Retail CBDC
Accessible to the general public — individuals and businesses. A digital equivalent of cash. Central bank issues directly (or via intermediaries). Key design questions: anonymity/privacy, offline capability, interest-bearing or not, programmability (spending restrictions, expiry).
Wholesale CBDC
Restricted to financial institutions for interbank settlement. Digital form of central bank reserves. Enables tokenised settlement of financial assets (DvP), cross-border payment improvements, and potentially replaces RTGS systems for specific use cases.

13.7 Wholesale CBDC and Cross-Border Settlement

While retail CBDCs address domestic payment needs, wholesale CBDCs (wCBDC) are specifically designed to transform cross-border payment infrastructure. The correspondent banking model — the current backbone of cross-border payments — is slow, expensive, opaque, and fragmented. Multiple central banks and international organisations are testing whether wCBDC-based platforms could solve these problems.

Key Wholesale CBDC Cross-Border Projects

🌉

mBridge

Multi-CBDC platform connecting PBOC (China), HKMA, Bank of Thailand, Central Bank of UAE, and BIS Innovation Hub. Tested cross-border trade finance payments. Using a purpose-built DLT platform ("mBridge Ledger"). Over $22M in pilot transactions completed.

🤝

Project Dunbar

BIS Innovation Hub + Reserve Bank of Australia, Bank of Malaysia, MAS (Singapore), SARB (South Africa). Prototype multi-CBDC platform for international settlements. Published technical report in 2022.

🇫🇷

Project Jura

Bank of France + SNB (Switzerland) + BIS. Tested cross-border settlement of tokenised assets (euro and Swiss franc wCBDC) and foreign exchange transactions using DLT. Third-party operator model.

🇨🇦

Jasper-Ubin

Bank of Canada + MAS (Singapore). Cross-border interbank payment using wCBDC with Hash Time-Locked Contracts (HTLC) for atomic PvP settlement. Demonstrated feasibility of blockchain-based cross-border PvP.

How wCBDC Could Replace Correspondent Banking

1

Tokenised CBDC on Shared Platform

Central Banks A and B both issue tokenised versions of their currency (wCBDC-A and wCBDC-B) on a shared multi-CBDC platform (like mBridge). Both tokens represent central bank money.

2

Atomic PvP Settlement

A cross-border payment triggers a smart contract: wCBDC-A is atomically swapped for wCBDC-B. Both legs settle simultaneously — no Herstatt risk, no correspondent bank needed, no pre-funded nostro accounts.

3

Near-Instant, Low-Cost Settlement

Settlement completes in seconds rather than days. No correspondent bank fees. Full payment transparency. Sender and receiver banks interact directly through the wCBDC platform.

The Vision: The BIS "Finternet" concept envisions a future where financial assets — including CBDCs, tokenised deposits, securities, and commodities — all exist on a unified financial ledger. This would enable instant atomic settlement of any transaction, in any currency, at any time — fundamentally transforming global payment infrastructure built around correspondent banking and SWIFT.

Challenges for CBDC Adoption

  • Financial Disintermediation: Retail CBDC could trigger bank runs if consumers move deposits to CBDC en masse — reducing commercial banks' funding base
  • Privacy vs AML: Governments need transaction traceability for AML/CFT; citizens demand privacy. Balancing these is technically and politically complex
  • Interoperability: Different national CBDC platforms need to interoperate for cross-border payments — requires international standards and governance agreements
  • Cybersecurity: A central bank CBDC platform is a high-value target — a breach could undermine monetary sovereignty
  • Monetary Policy Implications: Interest-bearing CBDC competes with bank deposits, affecting monetary transmission mechanisms

13.7b FATF Travel Rule for Crypto

The FATF (Financial Action Task Force) Travel Rule — originally Recommendation 16 for wire transfers — was extended to virtual assets in 2019. It is now one of the most operationally challenging compliance requirements facing the crypto industry, because crypto's pseudonymous architecture was not designed to carry the counterparty information that the Travel Rule demands.

Core Requirements

FATF Recommendation 16 — Crypto Application: Virtual Asset Service Providers (VASPs) — including crypto exchanges, custodial wallet providers, and digital asset brokers — must collect and transmit originator and beneficiary information for transfers above USD/EUR 1,000 (the "de minimis" threshold used by most jurisdictions).

Required Data Elements

PartyRequired Information
OriginatorFull legal name; account number or wallet address; physical address, national identity number, customer identification number, or date and place of birth
BeneficiaryFull legal name; wallet address or account number at the receiving VASP

Scope

  • Applies to transfers between VASPs (exchange-to-exchange, custodial wallet-to-custodial wallet)
  • Does NOT apply to non-custodial (self-hosted) wallet transfers — where there is no intermediary VASP to transmit the information
  • The "sunrise problem": compliance is only meaningful if both the sending and receiving VASP have implemented Travel Rule solutions — partial adoption creates gaps

TRISA Protocol

TRISA (Travel Rule Information Sharing Architecture) is an open-source protocol developed specifically for VASP-to-VASP Travel Rule compliance. It provides a secure, peer-to-peer messaging framework for exchanging originator and beneficiary data before a blockchain transaction is broadcast. TRISA uses PKI (public key infrastructure) for VASP identity verification and end-to-end encryption for data privacy. Adopted by major crypto exchanges globally.

Regulatory Landscape

🇪🇺

EU — MiCA & TFR

The EU's Transfer of Funds Regulation (TFR), effective 2024, mandates Travel Rule compliance for all crypto transfers above €0 — eliminating the de minimis threshold entirely. This is the strictest implementation globally, applying to even small transfers between VASPs within the EU.

🇺🇸

US — FinCEN

US FinCEN has proposed extending the Travel Rule to crypto transfers. Under existing Bank Secrecy Act rules, the Travel Rule already applies to crypto MSBs (Money Services Businesses). FinCEN has proposed lowering the threshold and extending requirements to non-custodial wallet transfers — though finalisation remains pending.

🌐

VASP Identification Problem

A fundamental challenge: unlike SWIFT, where a BIC (Bank Identifier Code) uniquely identifies any bank globally, there is no universal identifier for VASPs. A receiving wallet address does not tell the sending VASP which VASP (if any) is the custodian. Interoperability between different Travel Rule solutions (TRISA, OpenVASP, Sygna, VerifyVASP) is still developing.

🔒

Non-Custodial Wallets

When a user transfers crypto from a VASP to their own non-custodial (self-hosted) wallet, the Travel Rule cannot be enforced — there is no counterparty VASP to receive the data. Regulators are debating how to handle this gap; some jurisdictions require VASPs to verify the ownership of the unhosted wallet before allowing the transfer.

Systemic Significance: The Travel Rule for crypto is the AML framework's attempt to extend the "follow the money" principle — which works well in traditional banking via correspondent relationships — to a borderless, pseudonymous asset class. The technical and governance challenges of doing so at global scale remain substantial. The FATF's June 2023 review found that most jurisdictions have still not fully implemented the crypto Travel Rule.

13.7c mBridge Ledger — Technical Detail

mBridge is the most advanced multi-CBDC cross-border payment project globally. Understanding its technical architecture is essential for practitioners working in cross-border payments, central bank digital currency policy, or blockchain infrastructure.

Technical Architecture

mBridge Ledger — Custom DLT

The mBridge Ledger is a purpose-built distributed ledger — it is NOT based on Ethereum, Hyperledger Fabric, R3 Corda, or any commercial DLT platform. It was custom-designed by the BIS Innovation Hub in collaboration with the PBOC (China), HKMA (Hong Kong), Bank of Thailand, and the Central Bank of the UAE (CBUAE). This bespoke approach was chosen to give participating central banks maximum control over the architecture, governance, and performance parameters.

  • Consensus mechanism: PBFT-like (Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance) consensus — each participating central bank runs a validator node; transactions are confirmed when a supermajority of nodes agree
  • Permissioned: Only participating central banks (and authorised commercial banks) can join the network
  • Smart contract layer: Automates PvP FX settlement and liquidity management across currencies

Participants and Timeline

Founding participants: PBOC (China), HKMA (Hong Kong), Bank of Thailand, CBUAE (UAE), BIS Innovation Hub

2022 pilot: $22 million in real cross-border trade finance transactions completed between commercial banks in the four jurisdictions — the first multi-CBDC platform to complete real-value transactions

2024: Saudi Arabia joined as a full participant (Saudi Central Bank / SAMA), adding a fifth major currency to the platform

June 2024: mBridge declared Minimum Viable Product (MVP) ready — meaning the platform can process real commercial transactions, not just pilots. The MVP milestone is significant: it marks the transition from proof-of-concept to operational infrastructure.

Geopolitical Dimension

Outside Western Financial Infrastructure: mBridge is entirely built, governed, and operated by its member central banks — with no involvement from SWIFT, the Federal Reserve, ECB, or Western financial institutions. For participating countries (China, UAE, Thailand, Saudi Arabia, Hong Kong), mBridge provides an alternative cross-border settlement infrastructure that is outside the reach of Western sanctions or correspondent banking restrictions. This geopolitical dimension has attracted significant attention from policymakers in Western governments, who view mBridge as potentially enabling sanctions evasion. The BIS has emphasised that mBridge is designed for legitimate trade finance, not sanctions circumvention — but the architecture makes enforcement of Western sanctions technically impossible on the platform.

13.7d SWIFT and Blockchain Interoperability — Chainlink CCIP

Rather than being displaced by tokenised asset platforms, SWIFT has adopted a strategy of interoperability — positioning itself as the universal messaging layer that connects disparate blockchain networks, just as it connects disparate banking systems today. The partnership with Chainlink on CCIP is the most concrete demonstration of this strategy.

Chainlink CCIP — What It Is

Chainlink CCIP (Cross-Chain Interoperability Protocol) is a messaging and token transfer standard that enables data and assets to move between different, incompatible blockchain networks. In the fragmented blockchain landscape — where Ethereum, Avalanche, Polygon, Solana, and dozens of other chains each operate independently — CCIP provides a common translation and routing layer.

CCIP uses Chainlink's decentralised oracle network as the off-chain computation and attestation layer, providing cryptographic guarantees that a message received on one chain faithfully represents what was sent from another chain.

The Core Problem CCIP Solves

A tokenised bond on Ethereum cannot natively interact with a tokenised deposit on Avalanche. A smart contract on Polygon cannot atomically settle against a wCBDC on a permissioned fabric chain. Without an interoperability layer, each blockchain is an island — defeating the purpose of a unified tokenised financial ecosystem. CCIP provides the bridges between these islands.

SWIFT-Chainlink Pilot (2023)

1

SWIFT Message Sent

A financial institution sends a standard SWIFT MT/MX payment message through the existing SWIFT network — exactly as it would for any cross-border payment today. No change to the sending institution's existing systems or processes.

2

CCIP Translation Layer

The SWIFT message is received by a CCIP-enabled gateway. Chainlink CCIP translates the SWIFT message into on-chain instructions, identifying the target blockchain (Ethereum, Avalanche, or Polygon in the pilot), the token type, the recipient address, and the amount.

3

On-Chain Execution

CCIP executes the corresponding on-chain token transfer on the target blockchain. The receiving institution's tokenised asset is credited without the sending bank needing to know which blockchain the asset lives on, or having any direct blockchain integration.

4

Confirmation Back to SWIFT

Confirmation of the on-chain execution is relayed back through SWIFT, completing the end-to-end audit trail. From the sending institution's perspective, the transaction looks like a conventional SWIFT payment — but the settlement occurred on-chain.

Strategic Significance

🏦

Lowering Barriers to DLT Adoption

Banks with existing SWIFT integration do not need to build new blockchain interfaces for each tokenised asset platform. SWIFT + CCIP acts as a universal adapter — dramatically reducing the cost and complexity of connecting to the tokenised asset ecosystem.

🔀

SWIFT as Orchestration Layer

SWIFT's vision is to be the messaging and orchestration layer for tokenised assets — not replaced by blockchain, but evolved to sit above it. This mirrors how SWIFT already sits above individual RTGS systems — it does not hold funds, it coordinates their movement.

⚡

Multi-Chain Reach

A single SWIFT connection, combined with CCIP, could give a bank access to tokenised assets on any CCIP-connected blockchain — without maintaining separate integrations. In the pilot, SWIFT demonstrated connectivity to Ethereum, Avalanche, and Polygon from a single SWIFT API call.

📈

Tokenised Asset Market Growth

The global tokenised asset market is projected to reach $10–16 trillion by 2030 (BCG/ADDX estimate). As bonds, equities, funds, and real estate are tokenised on multiple chains, the need for interoperability infrastructure becomes critical — and SWIFT's 11,000+ institution network gives it a structural advantage as the orchestration layer.

Interoperability Architecture — The Bigger Picture: The SWIFT-CCIP pilot is one piece of a broader interoperability puzzle. Other components include: BIS's Nexus model (connecting instant payment systems), the Agora project (BIS + 7 central banks testing tokenised deposits and wCBDC on a unified ledger), and individual bank initiatives. The challenge is not building any one bridge — it is building governance frameworks that make multiple bridges work together in a standardised, legally recognised way across jurisdictions.
