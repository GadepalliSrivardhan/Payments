10.1 ACH Network Overview

The Automated Clearing House (ACH) network is a nationwide US electronic funds transfer system that processes large volumes of credit and debit transactions in batches. ACH is the backbone of consumer and business payments in the United States — used for payroll, vendor payments, bill payments, tax refunds, government benefits, and recurring consumer debits. Unlike RTGS systems that process payments one-by-one in real time, ACH batches transactions and settles net positions at defined intervals.

📊

Volume

Over 30 billion ACH transactions annually, representing more than $75 trillion in value. Includes both credit transfers and direct debits.

🏦

Participants

Over 10,000 financial institutions participate in the ACH network — commercial banks, savings institutions, credit unions, and thrifts.

⚙️

Two ACH Operators

FedACH (Federal Reserve) and EPN — Electronic Payments Network (operated by The Clearing House). Both operators inter-connect and exchange files.

📋

Governed by NACHA

NACHA (formerly the National Automated Clearing House Association) sets the operating rules, formats, and standards for the entire ACH network.

ACH vs Wire Transfer

ACH and wire transfers (Fedwire, CHIPS) serve fundamentally different needs. Wire transfers are real-time, individually settled, high-value, and generally irrevocable. ACH is batch-processed, net-settled, lower-value, and allows returns/corrections for several days after settlement. ACH is far cheaper per transaction — making it ideal for high-volume, lower-urgency consumer and commercial payments.

10.2 NACHA — Roles and SEC Codes

NACHA's Role

NACHA (Nacha — the Electronic Payments Association) is the self-regulatory body that governs the ACH network in the US. NACHA: (1) writes and maintains the NACHA Operating Rules — the legal framework all ACH participants must follow; (2) provides certification and compliance programmes; (3) manages the Standard Entry Class (SEC) code system; (4) enforces rules through fines and remediation; (5) drives ACH innovation (Same-Day ACH, real-time data solutions). NACHA does not operate the ACH network directly — that is done by FedACH and EPN.

SEC codes define the type of ACH transaction, its authorisation method, and applicable consumer protection rules. Each ACH entry must carry an appropriate SEC code.

10.2b FedACH vs EPN Interoperability

The ACH network operates through two competing but fully interoperable operators. Understanding how they coexist and exchange files is essential for understanding ACH routing.

🏛️

FedACH

Operated by the Federal Reserve Banks. Processes approximately 60% of US ACH volume. All Federal Reserve member banks have direct access. FedACH is the largest ACH operator by transaction count.

🏢

EPN — Electronic Payments Network

Operated by The Clearing House (TCH). Processes approximately 40% of US ACH volume. Primarily used by large commercial banks. TCH is owned by major US banks including JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Citibank, and Wells Fargo.

Mandatory Interoperability

NACHA rules require that FedACH and EPN interoperate fully. An ODFI using FedACH can send an ACH entry to an RDFI whose primary operator is EPN — and vice versa. From the originator's perspective, the operator distinction is invisible: they simply submit a NACHA-formatted file to their ODFI, and the routing happens automatically.

How Inter-Operator Exchange Works

1

ODFI Submits to Its Operator

An ODFI that uses FedACH submits its ACH file to FedACH. The file may contain entries destined for RDFIs that use EPN as their operator.

2

Operator Sorts by RDFI

FedACH sorts all incoming entries by the RDFI's routing number. Entries destined for RDFIs that use EPN are identified and packaged into an exchange file for EPN. Entries for FedACH-member RDFIs are retained for direct delivery.

3

Bilateral File Exchange

FedACH and EPN exchange files bilaterally each processing cycle. EPN delivers the FedACH-originated entries to its RDFI members; FedACH delivers EPN-originated entries to its RDFI members. This exchange happens multiple times per day.

4

Bilateral Net Settlement via Fedwire

FedACH and EPN calculate their bilateral net position with each other at the end of each processing cycle. The net amount between the two operators is settled via Fedwire Funds — real-time gross settlement in Federal Reserve accounts. This is separate from the multilateral settlement of each operator's own participants via NSS.

Practical Impact for Originators: Originators and ODFIs do not need to know — and typically do not know — which ACH operator their counterparty's RDFI uses. The routing is handled entirely at the operator level. This seamless interoperability is one of NACHA's core governance achievements, ensuring the ACH network behaves as a single unified system despite having two separate operators.

10.3 ACH Transaction Flow

Every ACH transaction involves: an Originator (the entity initiating the payment), an ODFI (Originating Depository Financial Institution) (the originator's bank), the ACH Operator (FedACH or EPN), an RDFI (Receiving Depository Financial Institution) (the receiver's bank), and a Receiver (the end beneficiary or payer).

1

Originator Creates File

Originator (e.g., an employer running payroll) creates a NACHA-formatted ACH file containing individual transaction records. The file is transmitted to the ODFI (their bank) by a defined cutoff time.

2

ODFI Submits to ACH Operator

ODFI validates the file and submits it to its ACH operator (FedACH or EPN) within NACHA's required submission windows. ODFI assumes responsibility for ensuring each entry complies with NACHA rules.

3

ACH Operator Sorts and Routes

The ACH operator sorts transactions by receiving bank routing number (ABA number in MICR). Entries for banks served by the other operator are exchanged through the FedACH/EPN interchange. Net settlement files are prepared.

4

RDFI Receives and Posts

RDFI receives the ACH file from its operator and posts the entries to receiver accounts (credit or debit). For credits, funds must be made available by the settlement date. RDFIs have return rights if entries are invalid.

5

Net Settlement via NSS

Net positions across all ODFIs and RDFIs are calculated and settled via the Federal Reserve's National Settlement Service (NSS) or directly in Federal Reserve accounts. Settlement is in central bank money.

NACHA ACH File Format

NACHA files follow a fixed-width text format with specific record types: File Header (1-record), Batch Header (5-record), Individual Entry Detail (6-record), Addenda (7-record) for remittance data, Batch Control (8-record), and File Control (9-record). Each record is exactly 94 characters wide. This rigid format (unchanged since the 1970s in its core structure) ensures interoperability across all ACH participants.

10.3b NACHA Risk Framework

NACHA's Operating Rules create a layered risk framework that assigns clear responsibilities and warranties to each participant. The framework is designed to ensure that the party closest to the origination risk — the ODFI — bears responsibility for entries that violate the rules.

ODFI Warranties

When an ODFI submits an ACH file to the network, it provides binding warranties to all other network participants. These are not merely representations — they are legally enforceable obligations under the NACHA Operating Rules:

📜

Rules Compliance

The entry complies with the NACHA Operating Rules in all respects — correct SEC code, correct format, correct timing, and appropriate authorisation type.

✍️

Proper Authorisation

The originator has obtained proper authorisation from the receiver. For debit entries, this means a valid written (or other NACHA-approved) authorisation agreement exists. For consumer debits, this is typically a signed authorisation form or recorded verbal consent.

🛡️

Not Fraudulent

The entry is not fraudulent — it reflects a legitimate, authorised transaction initiated by the identified originator for the stated purpose. The ODFI must have a reasonable basis to trust that its originator is not submitting fictitious entries.

⚠️

Liability on Breach

If any warranty is violated, the ODFI is liable for losses caused to the RDFI or receiver — including the full face value of fraudulent debits plus consequential damages in some circumstances.

Why ODFI Liability Matters: This warranty structure is what makes ACH debit possible for consumers. The receiver does not need to verify the originator's legitimacy directly — the RDFI relies on the ODFI's warranties. If a fraudulent debit is submitted, the consumer can dispute it with their RDFI, and the RDFI can claim against the ODFI. This creates a strong incentive for ODFIs to perform thorough originator due diligence before providing ACH origination services.

Return Rate Thresholds

NACHA monitors return rates by SEC code and originator. High return rates are a leading indicator of fraud, errors, or poor originator practices. NACHA has established the following thresholds:

Administrative Returns3% of entriesR02 (Account Closed), R03 (No Account), R04 (Invalid Account Number)NACHA investigation; ODFI must remediate originator or cease origination
Unauthorised Returns0.5% of entriesR05 (Unauthorised Debit), R07 (Auth Revoked), R10 (Customer Advises Unauthorised), R29 (Corporate Not Authorised), R51 (Item is Ineligible)Immediate NACHA investigation; potential suspension
Overall Return RateNo fixed thresholdAll return codesNACHA monitors for outliers; high overall rates trigger compliance review regardless of individual thresholds

NACHA Enforcement Mechanism

When NACHA identifies a potential rules violation — through return rate monitoring, complaints from ODFIs, RDFIs, or consumers, or its own audit activities — it follows a structured enforcement process:

  • Warning Letter: NACHA notifies the ODFI that a threshold has been exceeded or a violation identified, and requests a remediation plan within a defined timeframe.
  • Formal Investigation: If the issue is not resolved or is serious, NACHA opens a formal compliance investigation. The ODFI must provide documentation of its originator screening, authorisation records, and return rate management procedures.
  • Remediation Plan: ODFI and originator must implement a documented remediation plan — e.g., suspending problematic originators, implementing pre-notification requirements, or adopting enhanced authorisation verification.
  • Financial Fines: NACHA can levy fines of up to $500,000 per occurrence for rules violations. Fines may be assessed against the ODFI directly.
  • Suspension from Network: Repeated or egregious violations can result in the ODFI — or the originator — being suspended from the ACH network. This is the nuclear option and is rarely used, but the threat drives compliance.
Regulatory Coordination: NACHA coordinates with federal bank regulators — FDIC, OCC, and CFPB — on systemic fraud patterns and compliance failures. A pattern of ACH fraud at a bank may trigger a formal supervisory action by the bank's primary regulator in addition to NACHA enforcement.

10.4 Same-Day ACH and Next-Day ACH

Standard (Next-Day) ACH

Standard ACH processing has a T+1 settlement cycle: entries submitted today settle in the receiver's account the next business day. The ODFI submits files by a defined cutoff (typically late afternoon), the ACH operator processes overnight, and RDFI posting occurs by the morning of the next business day. This is suitable for payroll, pre-authorised bill payments, and other non-urgent transactions.

Same-Day ACH

Same-Day ACH, implemented in phases from 2016 (credits in Sep 2016; debits from Sep 2017; expanded limits in 2021), allows ACH transactions to settle on the same business day they are initiated. Key rules:

  • Three processing windows per day: Morning (10:30 AM ET cutoff, 1:00 PM settlement), Afternoon (2:45 PM ET cutoff, 5:00 PM settlement), Evening (4:45 PM ET cutoff, next morning) — varies by operator
  • Per-transaction limit: $1,000,000 (raised from $100,000 in March 2022)
  • Mandatory for RDFIs: All RDFIs must accept Same-Day ACH entries
  • Same-Day surcharge: ODFIs pay a surcharge to the RDFI to compensate for faster processing
  • Not available for IAT (international) entries or some high-risk SEC codes

10.5 Phixius — NACHA's Data Exchange Network

Phixius is NACHA's information exchange network, launched in 2020. It is a secure, peer-to-peer data exchange platform that enables financial institutions and service providers to share payment-related data in real time — before a payment is made. This enables a new generation of payment intelligence services that address fraud, compliance, and straight-through processing challenges.

✅

Account Validation

Verify that a bank account (routing + account number) is valid and belongs to the intended recipient before initiating a payment. Prevents misdirected payments and reduces returns.

🔍

Fraud Prevention

Share risk intelligence about suspicious accounts or counterparties across the network. Participating institutions can flag accounts with known fraud history.

📋

Payment Data Enrichment

Attach structured remittance data, invoice references, or other payment-related data to a transaction before it is sent — enabling STP at the receiving bank.

🌐

Open Ecosystem

Phixius uses open APIs (REST/JSON) and is accessible to banks, fintechs, and corporates. Governed by NACHA's trust framework for data security and consent.

Why Phixius Matters: ACH has historically had high return rates due to wrong account numbers and fraud. Phixius addresses the "validate before you originate" need — the same concept as CoP (Confirmation of Payee) in the UK and IBAN Name Check in SEPA.

10.5b Phixius as Information Exchange Platform

It is important to understand what Phixius is and is not. Phixius is NOT a payment rail — no money moves through Phixius. It is a pre-payment and post-payment data exchange platform, built on NACHA's trusted governance framework with API-based (REST/JSON) connectivity. Phixius participants query and respond to data requests; the actual payment flows through ACH, RTP, or other rails independently.

Services Available via Phixius

🔑

1. Account Validation

Verify that a routing number and account number combination is valid and currently active before originating a payment. Reduces R02/R03/R04 returns caused by invalid account details.

👤

2. Payee Name Validation

Match a payee name to a bank account — similar to the UK's Confirmation of Payee (CoP) or SEPA's IBAN Name Check. Prevents Authorised Push Payment (APP) fraud where funds are sent to a wrong or fraudulent account.

🛤️

3. Real-Time Payment Routing

Determine which payment rails a given account can receive payments on — ACH, Same-Day ACH, RTP (The Clearing House), FedNow. Enables dynamic payment rail selection at the time of origination.

📉

4. Return Prediction

Risk scoring to predict the likelihood of a return before origination. An ODFI or originator queries Phixius and receives a risk signal; if the score is high, they can choose not to originate or seek additional authorisation.

🔔

5. Fraud Intelligence Sharing

Participating institutions anonymously share fraud signals — known bad accounts, suspicious patterns, recent fraud events — across the network. Collective intelligence benefits all participants without exposing individual customers' data.

Governance and Adoption

NACHA launched Phixius in 2020 and adoption has been growing among large banks and fintechs seeking to reduce ACH return rates and fraud losses. NACHA operates as the trust anchor — it sets data standards, security requirements, and liability frameworks. Participants sign data-sharing agreements and consent management is built into the platform, ensuring data is only used for the purpose for which it was shared. No participant can use Phixius data for secondary purposes such as marketing.

Phixius vs Zelle / RTP: A common misconception is that Phixius competes with real-time payment networks. It does not — Phixius is a data layer that works alongside payment rails. A bank could use Phixius to validate an account and confirm the payee name, then use RTP or ACH to actually send the money. The services are complementary.

10.6 National Settlement Service (NSS)

The National Settlement Service (NSS), operated by the Federal Reserve Banks, provides settlement services for financial networks and payment systems that need to settle net interbank obligations in Federal Reserve account balances. NSS is the backbone settlement mechanism for ACH operators, cheque clearinghouses, card networks, and other private-sector multilateral clearing arrangements.

How NSS Works

A settlement agent (e.g., FedACH, an ACH clearinghouse, a card network) submits a settlement file to NSS specifying the debit and credit amounts for each participant financial institution. NSS verifies that the net settlement is balanced (total debits = total credits) and executes the settlement by adjusting each institution's account at its Federal Reserve Bank. Settlement is irrevocable once processed.

1

Settlement File Preparation

The settlement agent calculates net multilateral positions across all participants and prepares a balanced settlement file (total debits must equal total credits).

2

Submission to NSS

Settlement file is submitted to NSS via FedLine (Federal Reserve's secure access platform). NSS validates the file format and balance.

3

Federal Reserve Account Adjustment

NSS debits each net-debit participant's Federal Reserve account and credits each net-credit participant's account. Settlement occurs in central bank money.

4

Confirmation

NSS returns settlement confirmation to the settlement agent. Participants receive account notifications of the settlement debits/credits.

NSS Users

  • FedACH and EPN — ACH operators settle bilateral net positions
  • Check Image Exchange — Cheque clearinghouses settle cheque clearing net positions
  • Card Networks — Visa, Mastercard, Discover use NSS for interbank card settlement
  • CHIPS — Uses Fedwire (not NSS) for final settlement, but EPN uses NSS
  • Regional ACH Associations — Smaller regional clearinghouses use NSS for settlement
NSS Operating Hours: NSS operates Monday–Friday, 7:30 AM – 5:30 PM ET. Settlement must be submitted and confirmed within this window. Many clearinghouses schedule their final settlement runs to meet the afternoon NSS cutoff.

10.6b NSS Settlement Windows

NSS does not process a single batch at close of day — it operates across multiple settlement windows throughout the business day to accommodate the different clearing cycles of ACH, Same-Day ACH, and card networks.

WindowApproximate Time (ET)Primary Purpose
Morning Window~9:00 AM ETStandard (next-day) ACH credits processed overnight settle. Payroll, government benefits, and pre-authorised bill pay debits from the prior day's origination cycle post to accounts.
Midday Window 1~11:30 AM ETSame-Day ACH morning cycle settlement. Entries submitted by the 10:30 AM ET cutoff settle here — allowing same-day payroll and emergency B2B payments to post.
Midday Window 2~1:30 PM ETSecond Same-Day ACH cycle settlement and any deferred morning items. Provides a second intraday settlement point for higher-volume operators.
Afternoon Window~5:00 PM ETSame-Day ACH afternoon cycle (2:45 PM ET cutoff) settles. Also used by card networks (Visa, Mastercard, Discover) for their daily interbank net settlement.

Mandatory Participation and Advice Notifications

NSS is mandatory for clearinghouses and payment systems that use Federal Reserve accounts for final settlement. A clearinghouse cannot opt to use Fedwire directly for multilateral settlement — NSS is the designated mechanism. This ensures consistency, auditability, and finality of settlement across all clearinghouses.

When NSS processes a settlement window, each participating institution receives a Fedwire credit or debit advice notification confirming the amount settled. These advices are the authoritative record of settlement for accounting and reconciliation purposes.

NSS vs Fedwire: NSS and Fedwire Funds are both Federal Reserve services but serve different purposes. Fedwire is a real-time, gross, payment-by-payment settlement service for individual large-value transfers. NSS is a net, multilateral, batch settlement service for clearinghouses. A card network settling its daily interbank positions uses NSS; a corporate sending an urgent $50M payment uses Fedwire.

10.6c ACH Corporate Treasury — CTX and ERP Integration

For corporate treasury and accounts payable functions, one of the most powerful — and least understood — capabilities of ACH is CTX (Corporate Trade Exchange), the SEC code designed specifically for B2B payments with full remittance detail.

What Is CTX?

CTX is an ACH entry type that extends the standard CCD (Corporate Credit or Debit) entry by allowing up to 9,999 addenda records to be attached to a single payment. Each addenda record is 94 characters of structured remittance data. The remittance data is formatted using ANSI ASC X12 820 — the Payment Order/Remittance Advice electronic data interchange (EDI) standard — embedded within the addenda records.

📦

Up to 9,999 Addenda

A single CTX payment can carry remittance detail for thousands of invoices. A manufacturer paying a distributor $5M can include the detail for every individual purchase order that the payment covers — all in one ACH entry.

🔄

ANSI ASC X12 820 Format

The X12 820 transaction set is the EDI standard for payment and remittance advice. It allows structured fields for invoice numbers, purchase order numbers, discount amounts, and deduction codes — enabling fully automated reconciliation at the receiving company.

⚡

Straight-Through Processing (STP)

When a CTX payment arrives at the RDFI, the RDFI delivers both the payment and the addenda remittance data to the receiving company's ERP system. The ERP automatically matches each invoice line to an open receivable and closes it — zero manual intervention.

🏭

Major Use Case

Large manufacturers and retailers paying hundreds of suppliers via ACH with full remittance detail. Automotive OEMs, grocery retailers, and government agencies are among the heaviest CTX users.

Without CTX vs With CTX

Without CTX (Wire or CCD)

  • Payment arrives in beneficiary's account with minimal reference data
  • Payer must separately email or fax a remittance advice listing which invoices are being paid
  • Accounts receivable team manually matches the wire to open invoices in the ERP
  • With hundreds of invoices per payment, this process takes hours and is error-prone
  • Deductions (short-pays for damaged goods, pricing disputes) require additional manual investigation
With CTX

  • Payment and full remittance data (ANSI X12 820) arrive together in a single ACH transaction
  • RDFI delivers both payment and addenda data to the receiving company's ERP or TMS
  • ERP automatically matches each invoice number and purchase order to open receivables
  • Deduction codes in the X12 820 allow automatic posting of partial payments with reason codes
  • Accounts receivable team only reviews exceptions — unmatched items — rather than processing every invoice manually
Treasury Management Systems and CTX: Major TMS (Treasury Management System) vendors — Kyriba, ION Treasury, SAP Treasury, Oracle Treasury — all support CTX and ANSI X12 820 natively. A corporate deploying CTX typically integrates their ERP (SAP, Oracle, Microsoft Dynamics) directly with their bank's ACH origination platform, with the TMS handling the X12 820 formatting and the NACHA file construction automatically.
