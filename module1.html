1.1 Understanding Payment and Settlement

A payment is the transfer of value from one party to another in exchange for goods, services, or to fulfil a financial obligation. Payments can be made in cash, by cheque, electronically, or via card networks. In the modern banking world, the vast majority of payments are electronic.

Settlement is the final, irrevocable transfer of funds between financial institutions to discharge the obligations arising from a payment. When a customer instructs their bank to pay another party, the interbank settlement is the movement of funds between the two banks — typically through a central bank or clearing system. There is always a gap between the moment a payment is initiated and when it is finally settled, called the settlement lag.

Payment
The instruction or message that initiates the transfer. E.g., "Pay €500 to account X." The instruction is processed by the payment system.
Settlement
The actual movement of funds between institutions, typically in central bank money (reserves). Settlement is final and irrevocable.

Overview of Bank's IT Ecosystem for Payment Processing

A bank's payment processing infrastructure typically consists of multiple layers. The Core Banking System (CBS) maintains customer accounts and ledgers. Sitting above this are payment processing engines that handle message transformation, routing, validation, and compliance checks. Surrounding these are ancillary systems: sanction screening (OFAC, UN lists), AML monitoring, liquidity management, and reconciliation engines. The bank then connects to external market infrastructure — interbank clearing systems, central banks, and messaging networks like SWIFT.

🏛️

Core Banking System

Maintains customer accounts, processes debits/credits, holds the ledger of record for all account balances.

⚙️

Routes, transforms and enriches payment messages. Handles format conversion (MT↔MX), validation, duplicate checks.

🔍

📡

📊

Matches internal records with external nostro statements. Breaks are investigated and resolved daily.

Internal Bank Payment Flow Pipeline

Every outbound payment initiated by a bank customer travels through a multi-stage internal pipeline before it reaches the external clearing or settlement system. Understanding each stage is essential for payment operations professionals. Below is the end-to-end journey of a payment instruction from the moment a customer submits it to final settlement and reconciliation.

1

Customer Instruction

The customer initiates a payment via online banking, mobile app, branch counter, or API. The instruction contains beneficiary details (account number, bank code), amount, currency, value date, and payment reference. The front-end validates basic data quality — mandatory fields, IBAN checksum, BIC format — before accepting the instruction.

2

Front-End Banking Application

The instruction enters the bank's front-end banking platform (internet banking portal or mobile app backend). The system checks customer authentication, payment limits (single transaction cap, daily cap), account status (not frozen, not dormant), and available balance. A pre-authorisation hold may be placed on the customer's account to earmark the funds while the payment is in flight.

3

Payment Gateway / Orchestration Layer

The payment instruction enters the bank's internal payment gateway or orchestration engine. This system determines: (a) which payment rail to use (RTGS, ACH, SWIFT, domestic instant); (b) the routing path (which correspondent bank, which clearing system); (c) the message format required (MT 103, pacs.008, etc.). The gateway is the central nervous system of payment processing — it coordinates all downstream checks and actions.

4

Sanctions Screening (OFAC / UN / EU)

The payment details — payer name, payee name, beneficiary bank, intermediary bank, country, and payment reference — are screened in real time against sanctions lists. The primary lists include: OFAC SDN list (US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control — Specially Designated Nationals), UN Security Council Consolidated List, EU Consolidated List of Persons/Groups Subject to Financial Sanctions, and domestic lists (e.g., UK HM Treasury). A positive match generates an alert that freezes the payment and routes it to compliance for review. False positives are common and must be resolved quickly to avoid delays. Screening applies to all parties in the payment chain, not just the direct counterparties.

5

AML Transaction Monitoring

The payment is assessed by the Anti-Money Laundering (AML) transaction monitoring system. Rules and machine-learning models evaluate: transaction size relative to customer profile, unusual patterns (structuring below thresholds, rapid movement of funds, geographic risk), counterparty risk, and velocity (frequency of payments in a short period). Suspicious transactions generate Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) or Suspicious Transaction Reports (STRs) filed with the relevant Financial Intelligence Unit (FinCEN in the US, NCA in the UK). High-risk transactions may be delayed for enhanced due diligence review.

6

Duplicate Detection

The payment engine checks for duplicates against a rolling window of recently processed transactions (typically 24–72 hours). Duplicate detection uses a hash of key fields: amount, currency, beneficiary account, value date, and end-to-end reference. If a potential duplicate is detected, the payment is queued for manual review or automatically rejected with a notification to the customer. This protects against double-submission errors (human or system-generated) and replay attacks.

7

Liquidity Check

The payment engine queries the bank's intraday liquidity management system to verify that the nostro account designated to fund the payment has sufficient available balance. For high-value payments routed via RTGS, this is a real-time check against the settlement account balance at the central bank. For lower-value batch payments, it may be a projected end-of-day liquidity check. If liquidity is insufficient, the payment is queued (in an LSM queue or batch queue) until funding arrives, or escalated to the treasury desk for intraday funding action (e.g., drawing on an intraday repo facility).

8

Format Transformation (MT / MX)

The payment instruction is converted from the bank's internal format into the required external messaging standard. MT (Message Type) format is the legacy SWIFT FIN format — e.g., MT 103 for single customer credit transfer, MT 202 for bank-to-bank transfer. MX format is the modern ISO 20022 XML-based standard — e.g., pacs.008 for customer credit transfer, pacs.009 for financial institution credit transfer. The transformation engine enriches the message (adds BIC, LEI, charges codes, correspondent bank details) and validates the output against the schema. During the SWIFT migration period (2022–2025), banks must support both MT and MX formats simultaneously — requiring coexistence or in-flight translation.

9

SWIFT Gateway

The formatted payment message is sent to the bank's SWIFT interface processor (e.g., Alliance Gateway, Alliance Access, or a cloud-hosted SWIFT service bureau). The SWIFT gateway authenticates and encrypts the message, assigns sequence numbers, and submits it to the SWIFT network via the SWIFTNet FIN or InterAct service. The gateway also receives incoming messages and routes them to the appropriate internal processing system. SWIFT's Customer Security Programme (CSP) controls govern access to the SWIFT environment.

10

External Clearing System

The payment message arrives at the receiving bank or is routed through an intermediary clearing system. For domestic large-value payments this is the RTGS system (e.g., CHAPS, Fedwire, TARGET2). For cross-border payments it is the correspondent bank's processing system. The clearing system validates the message, queues it for settlement, and may perform further compliance checks. In a DNS system, the message is held and netted; in an RTGS system, settlement is attempted immediately.

11

Nostro Account Debit

When settlement occurs, the sending bank's nostro account at the correspondent or central bank is debited for the payment amount. Simultaneously, the receiving bank's account is credited. In RTGS this is instantaneous and irrevocable. The bank's internal Shadow Nostro ledger (the bank's own internal record of its nostro balance) must be updated in real time to reflect this debit. Failure to update the shadow ledger immediately leads to reconciliation breaks.

12

Reconciliation

At end of day (and intraday via MT 942 statements), the bank's reconciliation engine compares the shadow nostro ledger entries against the official nostro statement received from the correspondent (MT 940 end-of-day statement). Any discrepancy — a payment debited on the statement but not in the internal ledger, or vice versa — constitutes a reconciliation break that must be investigated and resolved within SLA. The reconciliation process is the final quality gate that confirms every payment has been properly accounted for on both sides.

Pipeline Principle: Each stage is a checkpoint. A payment that fails any stage is stopped, queued, or escalated — it does not proceed automatically. This sequential validation architecture is why a seemingly simple bank transfer can take seconds or hours depending on which flags are triggered along the pipeline.

1.2 Domestic Transaction Processing

Book Payments

A book payment (also called an intra-bank transfer) is a payment where both the payer and the payee hold accounts at the same bank. The bank simply debits one account and credits another on its own ledger — no interbank settlement or external message is needed. Book payments are instantaneous, free of external cost, and represent a significant portion of a bank's transaction volume. Examples include a customer transferring between their own current and savings accounts, or two customers of the same bank paying each other.

Local Payments Through Clearing Houses

When the payer and payee are at different banks, the payment must pass through a clearing and settlement mechanism. A Clearing House acts as a central counterparty or processing hub that receives payment instructions from member banks, validates them, and facilitates the exchange of funds. Clearing can be bilateral (two banks exchange directly) or multilateral (all banks send to a central hub, which nets positions and instructs settlement).

Role of Clearing House and Settlement Institutions

Clearing houses perform three key functions: (1) Clearing — exchanging payment instructions between participants; (2) Netting — offsetting obligations to reduce the gross amount that needs to be settled; (3) Settlement — final transfer of funds in central bank money. Settlement institutions are typically central banks (e.g., Bank of England for CHAPS, ECB for T2, Federal Reserve for Fedwire/FedACH). Central bank money is the ultimate risk-free settlement asset because it carries no credit risk.

1.3 Types of Payment Systems

Every payment system is built on three key elements: the Message (instruction carrying payment details), Clearing/Netting (exchange and offsetting of obligations), and Settlement (final transfer of funds). The architecture of these elements determines what type of system it is.

1. Deferred Net Settlement (DNS)

In a DNS system, payment instructions accumulate throughout the day and are not settled individually. At a designated point (end of day or multiple times per day), all obligations are netted across participants, and only the net balances are settled in central bank money. This dramatically reduces the gross amount that needs to be settled — often by 90%+ — reducing the need for intraday liquidity. However, DNS carries intraday settlement risk: if a participant defaults before end-of-day netting, all transactions involving that participant may be unwound, potentially causing systemic disruption. Examples: BACS (UK), older ACH systems.

DNS Risk: If a participant fails intraday, all their transactions may be unwound, causing cascading failures for other participants who had relied on those incoming funds.

2. Real Time Gross Settlement (RTGS)

In an RTGS system, each payment is settled individually and immediately across central bank accounts, in real time, on a gross (non-netted) basis. Each transaction is final and irrevocable the moment it settles. Because each transaction requires its own liquidity, RTGS systems demand significantly more intraday liquidity from participants. However, they eliminate settlement risk entirely — there is no accumulation of unsettled obligations. RTGS is used for large-value, time-critical, systemically important payments. Examples: Fedwire (USA), CHAPS (UK), TARGET2/T2 (Eurozone), BOJ-NET (Japan), Lynx (Canada).

RTGS Safety: Settlement is immediate and final. No end-of-day unwinding risk. The trade-off is high intraday liquidity requirements — banks must pre-fund their settlement accounts.

3. Hybrid System

Hybrid systems combine the liquidity efficiency of DNS with the settlement safety of RTGS. They use Liquidity Saving Mechanisms (LSM) — algorithms that continuously scan queues for offsetting payments that can be netted and settled together. This reduces the liquidity needed while maintaining RTGS-like finality for settled transactions. Examples: CHAPS (post-2017), Lynx (Canada), many modern RTGS systems incorporate hybrid features.

4. Real Time Final / Net Settlement System (RTFS/RTNS)

An emerging model combining real-time clearing with periodic gross settlement. Used in instant payment systems where clearing happens in real time but interbank settlement is deferred to specific windows (e.g., IMPS in India where NPCI clears instantly but RBI settlement occurs in windows). This provides a practical balance between immediacy and operational efficiency.

RTFS/RTNS — Architectural Distinction from Hybrid Systems

RTFS/RTNS is architecturally distinct from a Hybrid system, even though both seek to balance speed with liquidity efficiency. The critical difference lies in the separation of the clearing layer from the settlement layer:

Hybrid System Architecture

A single integrated system operates both clearing and settlement. The LSM (Liquidity Saving Mechanism) runs netting algorithms within the RTGS queue in real time. Netting and settlement are tightly coupled and occur continuously throughout the operating day. The settlement account at the central bank is updated immediately upon a matched netting event. There is no "clearing layer" separate from the "settlement layer" — they are one system with an embedded offset algorithm.
RTFS/RTNS Architecture

Two structurally separate layers operate independently. The real-time clearing layer (operated by a retail payment infrastructure like NPCI) processes, validates, and confirms payment instructions in milliseconds — providing instant payment confirmation to end users. The windowed settlement layer (operated by the central bank) runs at discrete intervals, settling the net obligations that have accumulated in the clearing layer since the last window. The clearing layer carries intraday credit exposure between settlement windows; the settlement layer extinguishes this exposure at each window.

NPCI IMPS as RTFS — A Detailed Example

IMPS (Immediate Payment Service), operated by NPCI (National Payments Corporation of India), is the canonical example of an RTFS architecture in production at national scale. It processes over 500 million transactions per month.

1

Customer-Facing Real-Time Layer (NPCI)

When a customer initiates an IMPS transfer, the sending bank submits the transaction to NPCI's clearing engine. NPCI validates the transaction, routes it to the receiving bank, and sends a confirmation to both banks — all within 30 seconds. From the customer's perspective, the money has "moved." The receiving bank credits the beneficiary's account immediately upon NPCI confirmation. However, at this point, no actual interbank funds have settled — NPCI has only confirmed the instruction and exchanged the message.

2

Accumulation of Net Positions

Between settlement windows, NPCI accumulates the net bilateral (and multilateral) positions of all member banks. Bank A may send 10,000 transactions to Bank B and receive 8,000, resulting in a net position. These positions are tracked in real time by NPCI's clearing system. The sending bank has a provisional liability to the clearing system; the receiving bank has a provisional receivable. Banks must maintain adequate liquidity buffers to cover potential net debit positions at the next settlement window.

3

RBI Settlement Windows (5 per day)

RBI operates five IMPS settlement windows per day: 08:00, 11:00, 14:00, 17:00, and 20:00 IST. At each window, NPCI sends RBI the net positions of all member banks. RBI debits and credits settlement accounts (held at RBI) accordingly. Net payers have their RBI accounts debited; net receivers are credited. Settlement is final and irrevocable at the moment RBI processes the window. Banks with insufficient RBI account balances at a window trigger a settlement failure — a serious event managed by NPCI's risk controls including collateral requirements and default management procedures.

4

Post-Settlement Reconciliation

After each RBI settlement window, banks reconcile their IMPS transaction logs against the RBI settlement statement. The net amount settled at RBI should match the sum of all transactions processed in the clearing layer since the previous window. Any discrepancy triggers an exception investigation between the bank, NPCI, and RBI.

1.4 Cross Border Payment Processing

Correspondent Banking — Nostro/Vostro Relationship

When two banks in different countries need to exchange funds, they typically do not have a direct relationship. Instead, they use correspondent banks — third-party banks that maintain accounts on behalf of other banks and facilitate cross-border payments. This is the foundation of the correspondent banking model that has existed for centuries.

The key account types are Nostro and Vostro accounts, two perspectives on the same relationship:

Nostro Account ("Our account at your bank")

Bank A holds an account at Bank B in Bank B's currency. Bank A refers to this as its Nostro account. Example: A UK bank holds a USD Nostro account at a US correspondent bank. The UK bank uses this to make USD payments.
Vostro Account ("Your account at our bank")

Bank B refers to the same account (Bank A's account held at Bank B) as a Vostro account. The same account — different perspectives. The Vostro account holder (Bank B) is the correspondent.

How a Cross-Border Payment Works

1

Customer Instruction

Customer at Bank A instructs payment to a beneficiary at Bank C in another country.

2

SWIFT Message

Bank A sends a SWIFT MT 103 (or pacs.008 in ISO 20022) to Bank B (correspondent), instructing payment to Bank C.

3

Correspondent Processing

Bank B debits Bank A's Nostro account, sends onward payment instruction to Bank C via local clearing.

4

Final Credit

Bank C credits the beneficiary's account. Payment is complete.

Nostro Reconciliation — Detailed Treatment

Nostro reconciliation is one of the most operationally demanding processes in a bank's payment operations function. The bank maintains an internal shadow nostro ledger — its own record of what it believes its balance to be at each correspondent. The correspondent bank simultaneously maintains the actual nostro account. Reconciliation is the process of comparing these two records and resolving any differences (called "breaks").

Types of Nostro Breaks

⏱️

Timing Differences

A payment instruction has been sent but has not yet been processed by the correspondent, or the correspondent has applied it to a different value date than expected. These typically resolve on the next business day and are the most common type of break.

❌

Failed Payments

A payment sent by the bank was not applied by the correspondent (e.g., due to beneficiary account issues, compliance holds, or a system error at the correspondent). The internal ledger shows a debit; the nostro statement does not — or shows the funds returned. Requires investigation with the correspondent and potentially a recall or re-execution.

📅

Value Date Discrepancies

The bank and the correspondent applied the same transaction on different value dates. This creates a temporary break that resolves at the later value date but can distort intraday liquidity reporting in the interim. Common with cut-off time mismatches or time-zone differences.

💸

Correspondent Fees

The correspondent charges maintenance fees, investigation fees, or FX conversion charges that are debited from the nostro account without a corresponding transaction in the bank's internal ledger. These "unexpected debits" must be identified, approved, and posted to the correct internal cost account.

👻

Ghost Entries

Entries that appear on the nostro statement but cannot be matched to any known transaction in the bank's internal records. Could be misrouted payments from another bank, systems errors, or in rare cases fraudulent entries. Ghost entries require urgent investigation and may trigger a fraud report to the correspondent.

🔄

Returned Funds

Funds returned by a beneficiary bank (unpaid, beneficiary account closed, invalid account) may be credited to the nostro at an unexpected time and amount, creating an unmatched credit. The reconciliation team must match this return to the original outward payment and update the internal ledger accordingly.

The Nostro Reconciliation Process

1

Receive MT 942 Intraday Statement

The correspondent sends MT 942 messages throughout the day — intraday statements listing transactions applied to the nostro account since the last statement was sent. Banks use these to update their shadow nostro in near-real-time and to detect any unexpected debits or credits promptly. MT 942 messages are typically scheduled every 1–4 hours during the business day.

2

Receive MT 940 End-of-Day Statement

At the close of the correspondent's business day, the correspondent sends an MT 940 — the official end-of-day nostro statement showing the opening balance, all transactions applied, and the closing balance for that value date. The MT 940 is the definitive record used for daily reconciliation. Banks may receive MT 940 messages for multiple correspondents in multiple time zones, staggering the reconciliation process across the evening and night.

3

Automated Matching Engine

The bank's reconciliation system automatically matches MT 940 entries against internal shadow nostro ledger entries using key fields: amount, currency, value date, transaction reference, and SWIFT end-to-end reference. Exact matches are auto-reconciled. Near-matches (same amount, slightly different reference or one-day value date difference) are flagged for human review. Unmatched entries on either side become reconciliation breaks.

4

Break Investigation Workflow

Each break is logged in the bank's case management system with a unique break reference, classified by type (timing, failed payment, fee, ghost entry), and assigned to a reconciliation analyst. The analyst investigates using: SWIFT messaging history (GPI tracker for cross-border), correspondent bank investigation portal, internal payment system logs, and if needed — direct SWIFT MT 195/196 query/answer messages to the correspondent. Each break must be resolved within the SLA — typically same day for high-value, 2 business days for standard value, 5 business days maximum before escalation to the Nostro Management team.

5

Resolution and Posting

Once the root cause of a break is identified, the appropriate corrective action is taken: a manual journal to post a correspondent fee; a recall instruction (MT 192) to retrieve a failed payment; a repair and re-submission of a rejected transaction; or a notification to the correspondent of an apparent error. The break is closed in the case management system once the internal ledger and nostro statement agree.

How SWIFT GPI Has Transformed Nostro Reconciliation

SWIFT GPI (Global Payments Innovation), launched in 2017 and now mandatory for SWIFT member banks, has fundamentally changed the transparency and speed of cross-border payment tracking — with major implications for nostro reconciliation.

Before SWIFT GPI

Banks sent payment instructions and had no reliable way to track their progress through the correspondent chain. If a payment did not arrive, the sending bank had to send an MT 195 investigation request — which could take days to receive a response. Nostro breaks from failed or delayed payments could remain open for a week or more. Reconciliation was largely backward-looking, relying on end-of-day MT 940 statements.
After SWIFT GPI

Every GPI payment carries a unique UETR (Unique End-to-End Transaction Reference) that persists through the entire correspondent chain. Banks and their customers can track payment status in real time via the SWIFT Tracker. GPI mandates that payments are credited on the same day (in most cases within hours). Each bank in the chain must provide confirmation of credit and cannot change the UETR. This same-day confirmation dramatically reduces the window during which a nostro break can exist — breaks are identified and escalated faster, and most timing differences self-resolve within hours rather than days.
GPI and Real-Time Nostro Reconciliation: Some leading banks now use SWIFT GPI's gCCT tracker data as a real-time feed into their reconciliation engines. When the GPI tracker shows a payment has been credited by the beneficiary bank, the shadow nostro is updated proactively — even before the MT 940 arrives. This "predictive reconciliation" reduces the number of open breaks at end of day and enables intraday liquidity reporting that is more accurate than was previously possible.

1.5 Components of Payment Processing

Role of Non-Banking Players

The payment ecosystem includes many non-bank participants who play critical roles in processing and facilitating payments. Card Networks (Visa, Mastercard, American Express, UnionPay) operate the infrastructure for card-based payments — authorisation, clearing, and settlement between issuers and acquirers. Payment Service Providers (PSPs) (PayPal, Stripe, Adyen) aggregate payment instruments and provide merchant-facing payment acceptance. FinTechs (Wise, Revolut, Ripple) offer alternative payment rails, often with lower costs and faster processing than traditional correspondent banking. Central Counterparties (CCPs) sit between buyers and sellers in financial markets, reducing bilateral counterparty risk.

Overview of Foreign Exchange from Payments Perspective

When a payment crosses currency boundaries, a foreign exchange conversion is required. The FX rate applied determines how much of the destination currency the beneficiary receives. Key concepts include: Spot rate (current market rate for immediate settlement), Forward rate (locked-in rate for future settlement), Cross-rate (derived rate between two non-USD currencies via USD), and Spread (bank's margin between buy and sell rates). In payments processing, FX conversion adds complexity: which party bears the FX risk? Who chooses the FX provider? SWIFT's GPI initiative introduced OUR/SHA/BEN charge codes to control who pays fees, and SWIFT gCCT (Global Currency Conversion) aims to provide transparent FX in cross-border payments.

1.6 Cheque Based Payment Processing

Cheque Truncation and Imaging

Cheque truncation is the process of stopping a physical cheque's journey at some point in the clearing cycle by replacing it with an electronic image or digital record. Instead of physically transporting paper cheques between banks and clearing houses, the digital image of the front and back of the cheque is captured and transmitted electronically. This dramatically reduces processing time (from days to hours), eliminates the risk of physical loss or damage, and cuts transportation costs. In India, the Cheque Truncation System (CTS) operated by RBI processes millions of cheques daily using image-based clearing.

Cheque Conversion

Cheque conversion goes a step further — a paper cheque is converted into an electronic ACH debit transaction at the point of presentment (e.g., at a retail counter or via remote deposit capture). The MICR (Magnetic Ink Character Recognition) data from the bottom of the cheque is read and converted into an ACH entry. The original paper cheque is voided and returned to the customer. This is common in the USA under NACHA rules for point-of-sale conversion (POS), accounts receivable conversion (ARC), and back-office conversion (BOC).

1.7 Automated Clearing House (ACH)

An Automated Clearing House is an electronic network for financial transactions — specifically batch-based credit transfers and direct debits. ACH systems process large volumes of low-value payments efficiently by batching transactions together and settling net positions rather than gross amounts.

Credit Transfers (Push Payments)

A credit transfer is initiated by the payer (the person sending money). The payer instructs their bank to push funds to the payee's bank. Examples include payroll, supplier payments, government welfare payments, and tax refunds. In ACH terms, the payer's bank is the ODFI (Originating Depository Financial Institution) and the payee's bank is the RDFI (Receiving Depository Financial Institution). The common NACHA entry codes for credit transfers are PPD (Prearranged Payment & Deposit for consumer), CCD (Corporate Credit or Debit), and CTX (Corporate Trade Exchange with remittance info).

Debit Transfers / Direct Debits (Pull Payments)

A debit transfer (direct debit) is initiated by the payee (the party collecting funds), with prior authorisation from the payer. The payee's bank pulls funds from the payer's bank account. Examples include utility bill payments, mortgage repayments, and subscription services. In Europe (SEPA), this is governed by the SDD (SEPA Direct Debit) scheme. In the US, ACH debits are used for the same purpose. The mandate (authorisation) from the payer is critical — it defines the amount, frequency, and duration of the debit authority.

Mandate Management System

A mandate is the authorisation given by a payer to a payee to collect direct debits from their account. A Mandate Management System (MMS) stores, validates, and manages these mandates. Key mandate attributes include: creditor identifier, debtor account details (IBAN/BIC), mandate reference, type (recurrent/one-off), amount (fixed/variable), and signature date. In SEPA, the e-Mandate scheme allows digital mandate setup. Mandate lifecycle management covers creation, amendment, cancellation, and expiry. Without a valid mandate, a direct debit is considered unauthorised and can be returned by the debtor's bank.

US ACH Authorization Rules

For ACH debit entries (pull payments), NACHA's Operating Rules require that the Originator (payee/creditor) obtain proper authorization from the consumer or business account holder before initiating any debit. The type of authorization required depends on how it was obtained and is codified in the Standard Entry Class (SEC) code attached to each ACH entry. Obtaining incorrect or insufficient authorization is one of the primary reasons for unauthorized return entries and regulatory sanctions.

Written Authorization

Written authorization is a signed paper or electronic document signed by the consumer or business owner giving the originator permission to debit their account. The authorization must: clearly identify the creditor/originator; specify the account to be debited (routing number and account number); state the amount (or how the amount is determined for variable amounts); describe the frequency of debits (one-time, recurring); and include clear notice of the consumer's right to revoke. The originator must retain the signed authorization for a minimum of 2 years after the last entry under the authorization. Written authorization is required for PPD (recurring consumer debits) and CCD (corporate debits).

Oral Authorization (Telephone)

Oral authorization is authorization obtained over the telephone, where the consumer verbally agrees to the debit. NACHA rules for telephone-authorized entries (TEL — Telephone-Initiated Entry) require: (1) The originator must either make an audio recording of the entire authorization (recording must be retained for 2 years) OR provide written notice to the consumer within a specified timeframe before the debit occurs; (2) The recorded authorization must contain all key terms: consumer's name, date of call, amount, bank account, effective date, and any recurring schedule; (3) TEL entries are restricted to situations where an existing relationship exists between the originator and consumer, OR the consumer has initiated the call to the originator. TEL authorization is commonly used by utilities, insurance companies, and subscription services for over-the-phone payment setups.

Internet Authorization (WEB Entries)

WEB (Internet-Initiated/Mobile) entries are ACH debit entries authorized via the internet or a mobile application. NACHA rules require: (1) The authorization must be communicated to the consumer via a secure web session (HTTPS) or mobile app; (2) The consumer must have a means to print or store the authorization (e.g., a confirmation email or downloadable PDF); (3) Originators must use commercially reasonable fraud detection systems — account validation (confirming the routing number and account number are valid and belong to the consumer) is mandatory at origination; (4) An annual re-verification requirement: for recurring WEB debits, originators must re-verify the consumer's account information at least annually to confirm the account is still valid and active. This is typically done via account validation services (micro-deposit verification, real-time account validation APIs, or the ACH Prenote process); (5) The authorization language on the website must meet specific disclosure requirements under Regulation E.

Point-of-Sale Authorization

POS (Point-of-Sale) entries are ACH debits initiated at a physical merchant terminal using a paper check as the source document. The consumer presents a cheque at the point of sale; the merchant reads the MICR data and initiates an ACH debit. Authorization is obtained by: (1) The consumer handing over the cheque at the point of sale (constituting implied authorization under NACHA rules); (2) The merchant voiding the original cheque and returning it to the consumer (or retaining it — rules vary by scheme); (3) Posted notice at the merchant terminal that the cheque will be converted to an electronic transaction. POS differs from standard ACH in that it occurs at the physical point of transaction, with same-day or next-day processing.

Mandate Lifecycle — US ACH Authorization

1

Creation

The originator obtains authorization from the consumer using the method appropriate to the SEC code (written, oral, internet). The authorization is recorded (audio, signed form, electronic log), and the originator's system creates a mandate record capturing all key parameters: consumer name, account details, amount, frequency, effective date, SEC code, and authorization method. The ODFI is responsible for ensuring the originator has obtained and retained valid authorization before submitting entries.

2

Amendment

If any key terms of the authorization change — new amount for a recurring debit, new account number, change in frequency — the originator must obtain fresh authorization. For some changes (e.g., de minimis amount changes within a range already authorized), prior notice to the consumer is sufficient. Material changes always require a new signed/recorded authorization. NACHA rules specify what constitutes a "material change" requiring re-authorization.

3

Cancellation / Revocation

A consumer has the right to revoke an ACH authorization at any time. Under Regulation E, revocation is effective when the RDFI or originator receives it with reasonable time (typically 3 business days) before the next scheduled debit. The consumer may revoke directly with the originator or via their bank (RDFI). Upon revocation, the originator must cease initiating entries under that authorization. If the originator continues to debit after revocation, the RDFI has the right to return the entry as unauthorized (Return Code R07 — Authorization Revoked by Customer).

4

Return for Unauthorized

When a consumer disputes an ACH debit as unauthorized, the RDFI may return it using specific NACHA return codes: R10 (Customer Advises Not Authorized — consumer claims no authorization was given); R29 (Corporate Customer Advises Not Authorized — for CCD/CTX entries); R07 (Authorization Revoked); R51 (Item Related to RCK entry is ineligible). The RDFI's return deadline for unauthorized consumer debits is 60 calendar days from the settlement date of the entry (under Regulation E). When the ODFI receives the return, it must pass the return back to the originator, who must investigate. Excessive return rates for unauthorized entries trigger NACHA compliance reviews and potential suspension.
