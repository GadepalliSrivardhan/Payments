9.1 CHIPS Overview

CHIPS (Clearing House Interbank Payments System) is a privately operated large-value payment system in the United States, owned and operated by The Clearing House (TCH) — a banking association and payments company founded in 1853, with CHIPS itself established in 1970. TCH is owned by the largest US commercial banks, including JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Citibank, Wells Fargo, HSBC, Deutsche Bank, BNP Paribas, and others. CHIPS is one of the world's largest private-sector payment systems and the primary clearing house for large-value USD transactions, particularly international dollar payments and correspondent banking flows.

🏛️

Operator & History

The Clearing House (TCH) — a private banking utility. Founded 1853 (TCH); CHIPS launched 1970. Regulated by the New York State Department of Financial Services (NYDFS). Moved from deferred net settlement (DNS) to today's prefunded hybrid model in 2001.

🏦

Participants

Approximately 43–50 large financial institutions including JPMorgan Chase, Citibank, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, HSBC, Deutsche Bank, BNP Paribas, Barclays, UBS, Credit Suisse, and other major domestic and foreign banks. Non-participants access CHIPS indirectly via a CHIPS participant acting as correspondent.

💵

Daily Volume

Approximately $1.8 trillion settled daily across approximately 500,000 transactions. Handles an estimated 95% of all international USD transfers processed within the United States, making it the dominant venue for cross-border dollar clearing.

🔄

Netting Efficiency

The multilateral netting algorithm achieves approximately 95% liquidity efficiency. $1.8 trillion in gross transactions is settled with only ~$50–90 billion in actual prefunded Federal Reserve money — meaning each dollar of prefunding supports approximately $20–36 of gross payment flow.

What CHIPS Specialises In

CHIPS is specifically designed and optimised for the following payment types:

Primary Use Cases
  • International USD payments — cross-border wire transfers in US dollars
  • USD correspondent banking — interbank settlement between US and foreign banks
  • FX settlement cover payments — the USD legs of large FX trades
  • Eurodollar transactions — USD deposits and transfers held outside the US
  • Trade finance settlements — letter of credit reimbursements in USD
Historical Evolution

Pre-2001 (DNS Model): CHIPS operated as a deferred net settlement system. Payments accumulated throughout the day; net positions were calculated at close and settled via Fedwire at end of day. This created settlement risk — if a participant failed before EOD settlement, its entire day's net position was at risk.

2001 — Prefunded Hybrid Model: CHIPS switched to requiring opening prefunds via Fedwire. Combined with continuous netting during the day, this eliminated credit risk while preserving the enormous liquidity efficiency of multilateral netting.
Key Advantage: CHIPS achieves approximately 95% liquidity efficiency through multilateral netting — meaning participants need only a fraction of the gross payment value actually prefunded in Federal Reserve money. This is the fundamental reason large banks use CHIPS for international dollar flows rather than routing everything through Fedwire.

9.2 Participation Requirements

CHIPS participation is restricted to a defined class of financial institutions. Unlike Fedwire — which is accessible to any Fed-eligible depository institution (approximately 9,000+ institutions) — CHIPS limits full membership to large institutions that meet specific legal and operational criteria.

Eligibility Criteria

To become a direct CHIPS participant, an institution must meet ALL of the following requirements:

🏛️

Legal Entity Requirements

Must be one of: a US-chartered bank (national bank or state-chartered bank); a US branch or agency of a foreign bank licensed under US law; or a US thrift institution (savings bank or savings association). Other entity types (credit unions, non-bank payment providers) are not eligible for direct participation.

🏦

Federal Reserve Account

Must maintain a reserve account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York (FRBNY). This is essential because CHIPS prefunding and end-of-day settlement occur via Fedwire transfers through FRBNY accounts. Institutions without a Fed master account cannot participate directly.

📋

TCH Membership & Agreement

Must complete the CHIPS Participant Agreement, agree to be bound by the CHIPS Rulebook (filed with NYDFS), and comply with The Clearing House membership requirements. This includes ongoing compliance obligations, regulatory reporting, and adherence to CHIPS operating procedures.

🔧

Technical Connectivity

Must establish and maintain technical connectivity to CHIPS (typically via SWIFT or a proprietary direct connection). Must have operational systems capable of managing intraday liquidity, submitting payment instructions, and processing CHIPS output messages within required timeframes.

Indirect Access — Smaller Institutions

The vast majority of US banks and credit unions do not meet the size and eligibility criteria for direct CHIPS participation. These smaller institutions access CHIPS indirectly via a CHIPS participant acting as their correspondent bank. The correspondent bank holds a CHIPS position and processes CHIPS payments on behalf of its respondent banks. The respondent bank's customers can still send and receive USD international wires that flow through CHIPS — but the respondent bank itself never appears in CHIPS; only the correspondent bank does.

Correspondent Access Example: A regional US bank in Ohio needs to send $50 million to a corporate client in London. The Ohio bank does not have CHIPS access. It sends a Fedwire or book transfer to its correspondent (e.g., JPMorgan Chase). JPMorgan Chase, as a CHIPS participant, then sends the CHIPS payment to Barclays (also a CHIPS participant), which credits the London account. The Ohio bank never appears in the CHIPS instruction — only JPMorgan Chase does.

9.3 CHIPS Message Format

CHIPS uses specific message formats for payment instructions and output notifications between the CHIPS central system and participant banks. The system is currently in transition from its proprietary format to ISO 20022 as part of the broader US payments modernisation initiative.

Current Format — COM (CHIPS Output Messages)

CHIPS currently uses a proprietary message format known as CHIPS Output Messages (COM format). This format has been used since the 1970s and is deeply embedded in participant bank systems. Key fields in a CHIPS payment instruction include:

SWIFT Connectivity

Most CHIPS participants submit payment instructions to CHIPS via SWIFT FIN messaging — typically using the MT 202 (bank-to-bank transfer) or MT 103 (customer credit transfer) message types, depending on whether it is an interbank or customer payment. Some large participants use a proprietary direct connection to TCH's processing infrastructure. SWIFT acts as the communications network; TCH's CHIPS system is the processing engine.

Migration to ISO 20022

As part of the US payments modernisation programme, CHIPS is migrating to ISO 20022 message standards — aligned with the Fedwire ISO 20022 migration and the global SWIFT ISO 20022 migration:

ISO 20022 Message Types for CHIPS
  • pacs.009 — Financial Institution Credit Transfer (interbank)
  • pacs.008 — FI to FI Customer Credit Transfer (underlying customer payment)
  • pacs.002 — Payment Status Report (acknowledgement)
  • camt.056 — FI to FI Payment Cancellation Request
Benefits of ISO 20022 for CHIPS

ISO 20022 brings significantly richer data capacity compared to the legacy COM format. Key improvements: structured beneficiary and originator data (vs free-form text); purpose of payment codes; LEI (Legal Entity Identifier) fields; full regulatory data (SEPA, AML); and improved straight-through-processing rates. Rich data enables faster compliance screening and reduces manual intervention.

9.4 Prefunding Model

The prefunding model is the cornerstone of CHIPS's risk management. Introduced in 2001, it replaced the old DNS model and eliminated ALL credit risk in the CHIPS system by ensuring that every payment entering the queue is backed by real Federal Reserve money already transferred to CHIPS.

Opening Prefund Process

Each business day, before CHIPS opens for payment processing at 9:00 AM ET, every CHIPS participant must transfer its required opening prefund amount to the CHIPS Prefunded Balance Account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. This transfer is made via Fedwire Funds Service. The CHIPS system does not open until it has received confirmation of the required prefunds from all participants.

1

Prefund Notification

Prior to each business day, CHIPS notifies each participant of their required opening prefund amount for that day. The amount is determined by CHIPS's prefund calculation methodology based on historical patterns.

2

Fedwire Transfer

Each participant sends a Fedwire transfer from their FRBNY reserve account to the CHIPS Prefunded Balance Account. This must be completed before the CHIPS opening time. Transfers typically occur between 7:00 AM and 8:30 AM ET.

3

CHIPS Opens (9:00 AM ET)

Once all required prefunds are confirmed, CHIPS opens and begins accepting payment instructions. Each participant's CHIPS balance reflects their opening prefund amount. Payments can now be submitted and the netting algorithm begins running.

4

Intraday Recycling

As payments settle throughout the day, participants receiving more than they send see their CHIPS balance increase. This liquidity is immediately available for further outgoing payments — creating a recycling effect that dramatically amplifies the purchasing power of each dollar prefunded.

5

End-of-Day Return

At day's end, after final netting, all participant balances in the CHIPS Prefunded Balance Account return to zero. Net debit participants transfer additional funds in; net credit participants receive funds out — all via Fedwire. The prefunded account closes at zero.

Prefund Calculation Methodology

CHIPS calculates each participant's required opening prefund based on a detailed analysis of historical payment flows:

Historical Flow Analysis

CHIPS analyses each participant's historical daily payment flows — specifically the inbound vs outbound payment patterns across multiple prior business days. The algorithm identifies:
  • Typical net outbound position early in the day (before large inbound flows arrive)
  • Intraday timing of inbound and outbound flows
  • Peak net debit positions during the day
  • Volatility and variance in daily flow patterns
The opening prefund is set to cover the estimated peak early-day net outbound position before significant inbound flows begin to arrive and recycle.
Asymmetric Requirements

Participants with consistently large net inbound flows — that is, they tend to receive significantly more than they send in aggregate — may have lower prefund requirements. This is because their inbound flows will arrive early and recycle quickly to fund their outbound obligations, reducing the opening balance needed.

Conversely, participants that are typically net senders early in the day — sending before large inbound flows arrive — need larger opening prefunds. CHIPS may adjust requirements periodically based on changing business patterns or risk assessments.
Credit Risk Elimination: Unlike the pre-2001 DNS model — where intraday credit was implicitly extended between participants with debit caps as the only control — the modern prefunded CHIPS model eliminates credit risk entirely. Every payment in the CHIPS queue is backed by real Federal Reserve money already on deposit at FRBNY. There is no intraday credit extended between participants at any point during the processing day.

9.5 Netting Algorithm — Technical Detail

The CHIPS multilateral netting algorithm is the system's core innovation and the source of its exceptional liquidity efficiency. The algorithm runs continuously throughout the processing day (9:00 AM – 5:00 PM ET), scanning all queued payments every few milliseconds for opportunities to settle groups of payments simultaneously using the minimum possible liquidity.

Why Netting Matters: Without netting, every payment would need to be fully funded individually — exactly as Fedwire operates. Bank A sending $100M to Bank B and Bank B sending $80M to Bank A would require $180M in funding. With bilateral netting, only $20M net is needed. With multilateral netting across dozens of banks simultaneously, the savings are even more dramatic — up to 95%.

Phase 1 — Bilateral Offset

The first phase of the algorithm continuously scans all queued payments for bilateral pairs — cases where Bank A owes Bank B AND Bank B owes Bank A simultaneously in the queue.

Bilateral Netting Mechanics

Scenario: Bank A has a queued payment of $100M to Bank B. Bank B has a queued payment of $80M to Bank A.

Without netting: Two separate settlements required — $100M from A's balance to B, and $80M from B's balance to A. Total liquidity consumed: $180M.

With bilateral netting: The algorithm detects the pair. Net position: A owes B a net $20M. CHIPS settles this with a single $20M debit to A and $20M credit to B. Both the $100M and $80M obligations are fully discharged. Total liquidity consumed: $20M — an 89% saving on this pair.

Phase 2 — Multilateral Netting (Cycle Detection)

Beyond bilateral pairs, the algorithm searches for payment cycles — chains of payments that form a closed loop where all participants can settle simultaneously without any one of them needing to fund the full gross amount.

🔺

3-Party Cycle

Classic triangle: A owes B $50M, B owes C $50M, C owes A $50M. Total gross: $150M. CHIPS detects the cycle — all three obligations can be discharged simultaneously with ZERO net cash movement. Each bank's balance is unchanged but all three payments are settled.

🔷

4-Party Cycle

A→B→C→D→A. CHIPS identifies that all four payments form a closed loop. If all four amounts are equal, the entire cycle settles with zero net movement. If amounts differ, only the net differences require actual balance transfers. Significant liquidity savings vs settling each leg individually.

⬡

n-Party Cycles

The algorithm searches for cycles of 5, 6, and more participants. The more complex the cycle, the rarer — but when found, the liquidity savings can be enormous. The algorithm weights potential cycle settlements by the total liquidity saving they would generate, prioritising the most impactful settlements.

⚡

Continuous Execution

The algorithm runs every few milliseconds throughout the day, not just at discrete intervals. As new payments arrive in the queue, they are immediately incorporated into the next algorithm run. Settlements occur continuously throughout the day — most payments do not wait for end-of-day processing.

Algorithm Priority and Queue Management

The netting algorithm also incorporates payment priority levels submitted by participants. Higher-priority payments move to the front of the queue and receive preference in matching. Participants actively manage their queues — adjusting priorities and timing of submissions — to optimise their own liquidity position and ensure time-critical payments settle early in the day.

Phase 3 — End-of-Day Final Netting

At 5:00 PM ET, CHIPS stops accepting new payment instructions and enters its end-of-day processing phase. A final, exhaustive run of the multilateral netting algorithm is executed across all remaining queued payments:

1

Queue Freeze (5:00 PM ET)

No new payment instructions accepted after 5:00 PM. The remaining queue of unmatched/unnetted payments is frozen for final processing.

2

Exhaustive Algorithm Run

Final multilateral netting algorithm performs an exhaustive search across all remaining queued payments — more computationally intensive than the real-time runs, maximising final netting opportunities before settlement.

3

Net Position Calculation

Each participant's final net debit or credit position is calculated across all settled and remaining payments. Participants with net debit positions must transfer additional funds; net credit participants will receive funds.

4

Fedwire Settlement (~5:30 PM ET)

Net debit participants send additional Fedwire transfers to the CHIPS Prefunded Balance Account by approximately 5:30 PM ET. This funds their remaining net obligations after the final netting.

5

Final Fedwire Payouts (~6:00 PM ET)

Net credit participants receive Fedwire transfers from CHIPS. All participant balances in the CHIPS Prefunded Balance Account return to zero. CHIPS closes for the day by approximately 6:00 PM ET.

9.6 CHIPS Closing Run Mechanics

The CHIPS closing run is a precisely sequenced series of events that transforms the day's accumulated queued payments into final settled positions and closes out all participant balances. Understanding the closing mechanics is critical for any payments professional managing a bank's intraday liquidity and end-of-day settlement obligations.

What Happens to Unmatched Payments at Close?

Any payment instructions still in the queue that could not be matched or netted by the final algorithm run — typically because the sending participant lacked sufficient balance and no offsetting inflows arrived — are returned to the submitting participant as unprocessed. These payments do not settle on that value date. The participant must either re-submit the next business day or find an alternative settlement route (e.g., via Fedwire). In practice, the vast majority of submitted payments settle within CHIPS; the closing algorithm is specifically designed to maximise settlement completeness.

Closing Run Risk: The critical operational risk during the closing run is a participant failing to send its required net debit Fedwire transfer by the 5:30 PM deadline. CHIPS rulebook provisions address this scenario — the defaulter's available prefunded balance is used to settle as many of their obligations as possible, and any residual is handled through defined loss allocation procedures. In practice, the prefunded model means a participant's net debit at end of day is a small fraction of their gross obligations — making defaults at closing extremely rare.

9.7 CHIPS Liquidity Management

Effective intraday liquidity management is essential for every CHIPS participant. The interplay between opening prefunds, intraday payment timing, and end-of-day settlement creates a complex liquidity optimisation problem that treasury and payments operations teams manage throughout each business day.

Sources of Intraday Liquidity in CHIPS

💰

Opening Prefund

The Fedwire transfer made before 9:00 AM to the CHIPS Prefunded Balance Account is the primary source of opening liquidity. Each dollar prefunded can support multiple rounds of outbound payments as inbound flows recycle through the system throughout the day.

🔄

Intraday Recycling

As participants receive inbound payments (other banks sending to them via CHIPS), their CHIPS balance increases. These inbound funds are immediately available to fund further outbound payments — creating a recycling effect. A bank that receives $2B before 11:00 AM can use those funds to fund $2B of outbound payments without additional prefunding.

📤

Intraday Top-Up

Participants can send additional Fedwire transfers to the CHIPS Prefunded Balance Account at any time during the processing day. This allows participants to increase their CHIPS balance if inbound flows are running slower than expected or if an unexpectedly large outbound payment needs to be funded.

📊

Real-Time Balance Visibility

CHIPS provides each participant with real-time balance information throughout the day — current CHIPS balance, queued outbound payments, expected inbound payments, and projected end-of-day net position. This visibility enables active, real-time liquidity management decisions.

Payment Queue Management Strategies

Sophisticated CHIPS participants actively manage their payment queues throughout the day to optimise liquidity usage and ensure timely settlement of important payments:

Inbound-Dependent Timing

Participants defer large outbound payments until expected large inbound payments arrive. If a bank knows it will receive $500M from Bank X at approximately 10:00 AM, it may hold a $400M outbound payment in queue until 10:15 AM — using the anticipated inbound to fund the outbound without needing additional prefunding. This requires accurate intraday cash flow forecasting and real-time monitoring.
Priority Management

Participants assign priority levels to queued payments. Time-critical payments (e.g., FX settlement legs with specific value dates, interbank obligations with contractual settlement deadlines) are assigned high priority to ensure they are matched first. Routine payments with flexible timing are assigned lower priority, allowing the system to optimise liquidity by settling high-priority items first while queuing lower-priority items to recycle inbound flows.

9.8 CHIPS vs Fedwire — Full Comparison

CHIPS and Fedwire Funds Service are the two pillars of large-value USD payment infrastructure in the United States. They are complementary — not competing — systems, designed for different use cases with different architectural trade-offs. Understanding how they differ, and why each exists, is fundamental to understanding US dollar clearing.

Interdependence: How CHIPS Relies on Fedwire

CHIPS and Fedwire are not simply alternatives — CHIPS is structurally dependent on Fedwire for three critical functions:

1️⃣

Opening Prefunding

Every CHIPS participant must transfer their opening prefund to the CHIPS Prefunded Balance Account at FRBNY via Fedwire each morning. Without Fedwire operating, CHIPS cannot open. This means CHIPS's 9:00 AM opening is dependent on Fedwire's earlier opening (9:00 PM the prior night — Fedwire is available when CHIPS participants need to transfer their opening prefunds).

2️⃣

End-of-Day Net Settlement

After the final CHIPS netting algorithm, all residual net positions are settled via Fedwire transfers. Net debit participants send Fedwire transfers to CHIPS; net credit participants receive Fedwire transfers from CHIPS. Fedwire is the ultimate settlement backbone that closes out all positions to zero each day.

3️⃣

Intraday Top-Up Transfers

Throughout the day, participants needing additional CHIPS liquidity send Fedwire transfers to top up their CHIPS balance. This intraday flexibility — made possible by Fedwire's near-24-hour operation — allows participants to actively manage their liquidity without over-prefunding at opening.

The Systemic Relationship: Fedwire is the ultimate settlement layer for the US dollar system. CHIPS adds a highly efficient multilateral netting layer on top of Fedwire specifically for the large-bank international dollar clearing use case. If CHIPS did not exist, all those payments would need to flow through Fedwire individually — requiring vastly more intraday liquidity and imposing higher costs on participants. Together, the two systems provide a tiered architecture: efficiency (CHIPS) layered on top of safety (Fedwire RTGS).
