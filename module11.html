Lynx RTGS System — Canada

Overview of Lynx — Canada's ISO 20022-native RTGS replacing LVTS, payment processing, liquidity management, and the four settlement mechanisms: UPM, LSM, RCM and RTM.

11.1 Overview of Lynx

Lynx is Canada's high-value payment system (HVPS), operated by Payments Canada under the oversight of the Bank of Canada. Lynx launched on 19 August 2021, replacing the legacy LVTS (Large Value Transfer System) that had operated since 1999. Lynx is Canada's first ISO 20022-native RTGS system, designed to be more resilient, efficient, and interoperable with global payment standards.

🍁

Operator

Payments Canada — the national payments infrastructure organisation. Settlement occurs in Bank of Canada accounts (ESAs — Exchange Settlement Accounts).

💵

Daily Volume

Approximately CAD 200 billion daily across ~30,000–40,000 transactions. Handles systemically important Canadian dollar payments.

📋

ISO 20022 Native

Lynx was built from the ground up to use ISO 20022 messaging (pacs.008, pacs.009, camt.052, camt.053). No legacy MT format support.

🔒

Collateral-Free

Unlike LVTS which required pledged collateral, Lynx uses a pure prefunding model — participants must hold positive balances in their Bank of Canada ESAs at all times.

Why Lynx Replaced LVTS

The LVTS (Large Value Transfer System) was a deferred net settlement system with collateral requirements. While effective, it was built on aging technology, supported only proprietary messaging formats, and was not easily adaptable to ISO 20022 or modern liquidity management needs. Lynx was designed to address these limitations with a modern, flexible architecture and native ISO 20022 support — positioning Canada's payment infrastructure for future interoperability with global systems.

LVTS vs Lynx: LVTS operated as a DNS with collateral-backed credit limits. Lynx operates as a true RTGS with prefunding — each payment requires a positive balance in the sender's ESA. This eliminates credit risk while requiring participants to manage intraday liquidity more actively.

11.2 Lynx Payments Processing

Direct Participants

Direct participants in Lynx must be eligible financial institutions — primarily Schedule I and Schedule II banks, and credit unions with Bank of Canada ESAs. As of launch, Lynx has approximately 15 direct participants. Smaller financial institutions access Lynx indirectly through a direct participant (similar to correspondent banking). Each direct participant must hold an Exchange Settlement Account (ESA) at the Bank of Canada, which serves as the settlement account for all Lynx transactions.

All Lynx messages use ISO 20022 XML. Key messages:

Payment Finality

Lynx provides immediate, irrevocable finality for each settled payment. Once a payment is processed by Lynx and the sending participant's ESA is debited, settlement is final under the Payment Clearing and Settlement Act (PCSA). The PCSA provides statutory finality — settled payments cannot be reversed or set aside by insolvency proceedings. This legal finality is critical for the systemic safety of Canada's payment infrastructure.

11.3 Lynx Liquidity Management

Collateral-Free Prefunding

Lynx operates on a pure prefunding model. Participants must maintain positive ESA balances at the Bank of Canada throughout the day to fund their payment obligations. There is no intraday credit extension and no collateral pledging mechanism (unlike LVTS or some RTGS systems that offer repo facilities). The Bank of Canada does provide an Overnight Standing Facility — participants can borrow overnight at the Bank Rate — but intraday credit is not available within Lynx itself.

Intraday Liquidity Recycling

The Lynx architecture is designed to maximise intraday liquidity recycling: as participants receive incoming payments, their ESA balances increase, enabling them to send further payments without needing additional external funding. The LSM (Liquidity Saving Mechanism) described below is the primary tool for achieving this recycling effect efficiently.

Bank of Canada Intraday Repo

While Lynx itself doesn't provide intraday credit, the Bank of Canada operates an Intraday Liquidity Facility (ILF) under which direct clearers can obtain intraday liquidity by pledging eligible securities (Government of Canada bonds and bills) in a repo transaction. This ILF is separate from Lynx but provides a backstop liquidity mechanism for participants who need intraday funding.

11.4 UPM, LSM, RCM and RTM — The Four Mechanisms

Lynx incorporates four distinct payment processing mechanisms, each designed for different payment priorities and liquidity management strategies. Participants assign payments to mechanisms based on urgency and their desire to benefit from offsetting/netting efficiency.

1. UPM — Urgent Payment Mechanism

UPM is the highest-priority queue in Lynx. Payments assigned to UPM are settled immediately and individually on a gross basis as soon as sufficient funds are available in the sending participant's ESA. UPM provides the fastest settlement — equivalent to pure RTGS with no queueing or netting. Used for: time-critical, high-value payments where immediacy is paramount (e.g., money market transactions with same-day settlement deadlines, urgent corporate payments).

2. LSM — Liquidity Saving Mechanism

LSM is the liquidity-efficient queue. Payments placed in LSM are held and processed by the Lynx algorithm which continuously scans for bilateral and multilateral offsets — pairs or cycles of payments between participants that can be settled simultaneously using only the net difference in liquidity. LSM significantly reduces the gross liquidity consumed. It is analogous to the queue-based netting found in systems like CHIPS and the ECB's TARGET2. Most standard-priority large-value payments are submitted to LSM to benefit from its liquidity saving properties.

3. RCM — Regular Credit Mechanism

RCM is the standard, lower-priority queue. Payments in RCM are processed in FIFO (First In, First Out) order, on a gross basis, as ESA funds become available. RCM sits between UPM (immediate) and LSM (optimised) in terms of priority. Banks may use RCM for payments that are important but don't need the immediacy of UPM and where the volume is insufficient to benefit significantly from LSM netting. RCM ensures all queued payments are eventually settled as liquidity flows in.

4. RTM — Real-Time Mechanism

RTM supports retail payment system (RPS) settlement — the settlement of net positions from ancillary systems. When Canada's retail payment systems (like the Automated Clearing Settlement System, ACSS) calculate their end-of-cycle net positions, these positions are settled in Lynx via the RTM. RTM handles both bilateral net positions and multilateral net positions submitted by clearing agents. It provides a clean interface between Lynx and ancillary systems, ensuring that retail payment settlement flows efficiently through the RTGS infrastructure.

11.5 Canadian Payments Ecosystem

Lynx sits at the top of Canada's payment hierarchy. Below it are several retail and mass payment systems:

⚡

Interac e-Transfer

Canada's dominant consumer real-time payment service. Uses email/mobile as proxies for account lookup. Operated by Interac Corp. ~$250 billion transferred annually. Settlement via ACSS.

🔄

AFT — Automated Funds Transfer

Canada's ACH equivalent — batch credit and debit transfers. Used for payroll, pre-authorised debits (utility bills, mortgage payments). Governed by Payments Canada Rule E2.

📄

ACSS — Automated Clearing Settlement System

Clears retail items (AFT, cheques, image-based items). Net positions from ACSS settle in Lynx via RTM each business day. Replacing with ACSS Modernisation project.

🚀

RTR — Real-Time Rail (Upcoming)

Canada's planned instant payment system (equivalent to RTP/FedNow in US). Will provide 24/7 real-time credit transfers with immediate finality. Under development by Payments Canada.

Payment Canada's Modernisation Programme: Alongside Lynx, Payments Canada is modernising the entire Canadian payments ecosystem — upgrading ACSS, building the RTR (Real-Time Rail), and implementing ISO 20022 across all systems. Canada's approach emphasises safety and stakeholder consultation, with phased implementation over several years.
