8.1 Overview of Regulatory Structure in the USA

The United States operates one of the most complex and fragmented financial regulatory frameworks in the world. Unlike most countries that use a single financial regulator, the US employs a dual banking system and a multi-agency oversight model with overlapping jurisdictions at both the federal and state levels. The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010, enacted in response to the 2008 financial crisis, substantially reorganised US financial oversight — creating the CFPB, establishing FSOC, and designating systemically important financial market utilities (SIFMUs) for enhanced supervision.

Dodd-Frank Act — Payment System Implications

  • Title VIII — Payment, Clearing, and Settlement Supervision: Established enhanced risk management standards for SIFMUs including financial resources, liquidity, governance, and recovery/resolution planning requirements.
  • Durbin Amendment (Section 1075): Directed the Federal Reserve to limit debit card interchange fees. Resulting Regulation II caps interchange at $0.21 + 0.05% per transaction for large issuers (over $10B assets).
  • CFPB Creation: Gave consumers comprehensive protection for payment products including a right to receive remittance disclosures, error resolution rights, and limits on prepaid card fees.
  • Volcker Rule: Restricts proprietary trading by banks, reducing market-making capacity and affecting liquidity in securities markets that settlement systems rely upon.
Dual Banking System: US banks may be chartered federally (national banks — OCC) or at the state level (state banks — state regulators). State banks that join the Federal Reserve System are also supervised by the Fed. This creates potentially three overlapping layers of supervision. The result is a more complex compliance environment than single-regulator jurisdictions like the UK (PRA/FCA) or EU (ECB/national competent authorities).

8.2 USA Payments Landscape

The United States operates a rich and diverse payments ecosystem combining Federal Reserve-operated public infrastructure with private sector networks. The US lacks a single unified retail payment infrastructure — instead, multiple complementary systems serve different payment needs, from large-value interbank transfers to instant retail payments and consumer P2P.

🏛️

Fedwire Funds Service

Federal Reserve RTGS for large-value, time-critical USD payments. ~$4 trillion settled daily across ~650,000 transactions. Operated by the Fed, accessible to ~9,000 financial institutions.

🏦

CHIPS

Clearing House Interbank Payments System — private sector large-value system by The Clearing House. ~50 participant banks, ~$1.8 trillion daily. Multilateral netting with Fedwire final settlement.

🔄

ACH Network

Automated Clearing House — batch EFT network with two operators: FedACH and EPN (TCH). ~30 billion transactions per year covering payroll, bill pay, vendor payments, and consumer debits.

⚡

RTP Network

Real-Time Payments by The Clearing House. Launched 2017, 24/7/365, up to $1M per transaction. Covers ~90% of US demand deposit accounts via participating banks.

⚡

FedNow

Federal Reserve's own instant payment service, launched July 2023. 24/7/365 real-time gross settlement, $500,000 transaction limit. Directly competes with RTP, open to all Fed-eligible institutions.

📱

Zelle

P2P network operated by Early Warning Services (owned by major US banks). Integrated into banking apps — processed over $800B in 2023. Uses RTP or ACH rails depending on sending/receiving bank capabilities.

💳

Card Networks

Visa, Mastercard (four-party open loop), American Express and Discover (three-party closed loop). Process billions of debit/credit card transactions. PIN debit uses PIN debit networks (STAR, NYCE, Pulse).

📋

Check 21 / Image Clearing

Check Clearing for the 21st Century Act (2004) enabled electronic check image exchange. ~6 billion checks annually remain, declining rapidly. Federal Reserve Check Services clears check images.

8.3 Overview of Fedwire Funds Service

The Fedwire Funds Service is the United States' primary large-value Real-Time Gross Settlement (RTGS) system, operated by the twelve Federal Reserve Banks. Each payment settles individually, immediately, and irrevocably at the moment it is processed — there is no netting, no end-of-day batch, and no reversal once settlement occurs. This makes Fedwire the backbone of the US financial system for time-critical, large-value dollar payments.

⚡

Real-Time Gross Settlement

Every Fedwire payment settles individually and instantly. No batching or netting. Settlement is final and irrevocable at the moment of processing. Eliminates intraday credit risk between participants.

🏛️

Federal Reserve Operated

Operated by the 12 Federal Reserve Banks collectively. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York plays a central processing role given New York's dominance in US wholesale financial markets.

💰

Scale — ~$4 Trillion Daily

Processes approximately $4 trillion in value daily — roughly equivalent to the entire US annual GDP processed every single business day. Critical for interbank settlement, fed funds market, and correspondent banking.

🏦

Broad Participation

~9,000 financial institutions hold Federal Reserve accounts and can use Fedwire. Includes commercial banks, savings institutions, credit unions, and US branches of foreign banks.

Account Types on Fedwire

Financial institutions that participate in Fedwire hold accounts at their local Federal Reserve Bank. These are called master accounts (formally Reserve Accounts or Fed accounts). Balances in these accounts represent claims on the Federal Reserve — they are the highest form of settlement asset in USD, as they carry no credit risk (the Fed cannot default on USD obligations).

  • Master Account: Primary account held at a Reserve Bank. Used for all Fed services including Fedwire, ACH, and minimum reserve requirements. Earns interest on excess reserves (IOER/IORB).
  • Joint Account: Shared account arrangement where multiple institutions pool liquidity for settlement purposes.
  • Pass-Through Reserve Account: Account maintained by a correspondent bank on behalf of smaller respondent banks for reserve and settlement purposes.

Settlement Finality

Under Regulation J (12 CFR Part 210), a Fedwire funds transfer is final at the moment the Federal Reserve Bank credits the receiving institution's account. This means:

  • Payment cannot be recalled or reversed after settlement occurs
  • Receiving bank can release funds to its customer immediately upon receipt
  • No counterparty risk for the receiving bank — the Fed's credit is risk-free
  • Provides legal certainty under the UCC (Uniform Commercial Code) Article 4A framework for funds transfers

Fedwire Message Format and ISO 20022 Migration

Fedwire historically uses a proprietary fixed-length tag-value format. Key identifiers include the IMAD (Input Message Accountability Data) and OMAD (Output Message Accountability Data) — unique identifiers that allow tracking of individual payments. The Fed has announced migration to ISO 20022 messaging aligned with the broader industry migration and CHIPS transition.

8.4 Fedwire Securities Service

The Fedwire Securities Service is the Federal Reserve's book-entry system for the safekeeping, transfer, and settlement of US government and federal agency securities. It is a critical component of US financial market infrastructure — providing the settlement backbone for US Treasury securities, which are the world's pre-eminent safe asset and the benchmark for global risk-free rates.

What is Book-Entry Settlement?

Book-entry means securities exist only as electronic records — there are no physical certificates. Ownership and transfer are recorded by simple debit/credit entries on the Fedwire Securities Service ledger. The Federal Reserve Banks act as the fiscal agent for the US Treasury and as custodian for the book-entry securities.

🏛️

US Treasury Securities

T-Bills (short-term, discount), T-Notes (2–10 year), T-Bonds (20–30 year), TIPS (inflation-indexed), FRNs (floating rate notes). All issued and settled via Fedwire Securities. The US Treasury conducts auctions via TreasuryDirect; primary dealers receive allocations settled via Fedwire.

🏦

Agency Securities

Securities issued by US government-sponsored enterprises (GSEs): Fannie Mae (FNMA), Freddie Mac (FHLMC), Federal Home Loan Banks, Farm Credit System. Also Federal Reserve Bank securities. All settled via Fedwire Securities in book-entry form.

🔄

DvP Settlement

Delivery versus Payment (DvP) — securities and funds transfer simultaneously. When a buyer purchases Treasuries, Fedwire Securities moves the securities to the buyer's custody account while simultaneously Fedwire Funds (or an interlinked mechanism) debits the buyer's cash account. Eliminates principal risk.

🔒

Federal Reserve as Custodian

The Fed acts as custodian for foreign central banks and international official institutions holding US Treasury securities as foreign exchange reserves. This is one of the largest custodial relationships in the world — foreign central banks hold over $7 trillion in US Treasuries.

Fedwire Securities Operating Model

Fedwire Securities operates through a two-tier structure:

1

Federal Reserve Level (Tier 1)

Federal Reserve Banks hold securities in book-entry form directly for large institutions (primary dealers, large commercial banks, foreign central banks, government agencies). These holders receive securities credited directly to their Fedwire Securities accounts. Transfers between Tier 1 holders settle in real time via the Fedwire Securities system.

2

Depository Trust Company / Custodian Level (Tier 2)

DTC (part of DTCC) holds a large omnibus account at Fedwire Securities on behalf of its broker-dealer and institutional investor participants. DTC handles intra-DTC securities settlement for the broader market — most retail and institutional Treasury transactions settle within DTC using Fedwire Securities as the ultimate custodian.

3

Real-Time Gross Settlement of Securities

Securities transfers on Fedwire Securities settle on a gross, real-time basis — each transfer settles immediately and irrevocably, just like Fedwire Funds. The linked DvP mechanism ensures securities and cash move simultaneously, eliminating principal risk in Treasury market transactions.

Scale of Fedwire Securities

Fedwire Securities processes approximately $10–12 trillion in securities transfers on peak days (for example, at Treasury auction settlement dates), making it one of the largest securities settlement systems in the world. The total value of book-entry securities on Fedwire Securities exceeds $30 trillion, reflecting the enormous size of the US government securities market.

8.5 Fedwire Support to SWIFT GPI

SWIFT GPI (Global Payments Innovation) has transformed cross-border correspondent banking by providing end-to-end payment tracking, speed commitments, and transparency on fees and FX rates. For USD cross-border payments, Fedwire plays an essential role as the domestic USD settlement backbone that ultimately settles the US-side leg of international dollar transactions.

SWIFT GPI Overview

SWIFT GPI introduced several key elements for cross-border payments:

  • UETR (Unique End-to-End Transaction Reference): A UUID (128-bit unique identifier) assigned to each GPI payment at origination and carried through all correspondent banks in the payment chain. Enables end-to-end tracking via the SWIFT Tracker.
  • gpi Tracker: Cloud-based database where each correspondent bank in the chain confirms processing and forwards the UETR. Originating banks and their customers can see real-time status of where the payment is in the correspondent chain.
  • Speed Commitments: GPI member banks commit to same-day use of funds for payments received before cutoff times. For USD payments, this typically means completion within hours rather than days.
  • Fee Transparency: Each correspondent bank must report what fee they deducted, so the beneficiary receives the net amount and the originator knows the deduction chain.

Role of Fedwire in USD Cross-Border Payments

For cross-border USD payments using correspondent banking, the payment flow typically involves:

1

Non-US Bank Initiates via Correspondent

A non-US bank (e.g., a German bank) sends a SWIFT MT 103 (or pacs.008 in ISO 20022) payment to its US correspondent bank (e.g., JPMorgan or Citibank). The message carries the UETR and payment details. The German bank's nostro account at the US correspondent will be debited.

2

US Correspondent Processes via Fedwire or CHIPS

The US correspondent bank receives the SWIFT instruction and executes a USD payment to the beneficiary's bank. For large-value or time-critical payments, this is done via Fedwire Funds (RTGS). For net-settled interbank payments, it may route via CHIPS. The SWIFT UETR is carried in the payment reference data.

3

Fedwire Settles Final USD Leg

Fedwire processes the payment RTGS — debiting the sending bank's Fed master account and crediting the receiving bank's Fed master account. Settlement is final and irrevocable. The UETR can be referenced via the Fedwire payment reference fields.

4

GPI Tracker Updated

Each bank in the chain updates the SWIFT GPI Tracker with payment status. Originating bank and beneficiary bank can see the full chain of events, timing, fees, and FX rates applied. The UETR links the SWIFT GPI record to the underlying Fedwire settlement.
