3.1 Recalling Herstatt Risk — The Origin of FX Settlement Risk

On 26 June 1974, German banking regulators closed Bankhaus Herstatt, a mid-sized German bank based in Cologne, after discovering it had accumulated enormous speculative FX losses. The timing of the closure was critical and became a defining moment in payment system history.

German banks had already irrevocably paid Deutsche Marks (DEM) to Herstatt during Frankfurt banking hours. However, the closure happened after Frankfurt closed but before New York opened. The US counterparties — Chase Manhattan and others — were expecting to receive their USD legs of the FX trades. When Herstatt's New York correspondent, Chase Manhattan, was instructed to suspend payments, those counterparties lost their USD legs entirely. They had delivered DEM but received no USD in return.

The Core Problem: In an FX trade, two currencies settle in two different countries, in two different time zones, at two different times. This creates a window of exposure where one party has irrevocably paid but has not yet received. This is called principal risk — the entire value of the trade is at risk, not just the profit.

Principal Risk vs Replacement Cost Risk

Principal Risk

The risk of losing the FULL VALUE of the transaction — the entire amount of currency delivered. In the Herstatt case, US counterparties lost 100% of the USD they were owed. This is the risk that CLS was specifically created to eliminate through PvP settlement.
Replacement Cost Risk

The risk of losing only the profit/gain — the cost to replace the trade at current market prices if the counterparty defaults before settlement date. This is the much smaller pre-settlement exposure that remains even with CLS (since it arises before settlement, not during it).

The Herstatt failure caused widespread disruption in the interbank market, triggered a global liquidity crisis in the USD clearing system, and led directly to the creation of the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision. It also galvanised the industry to find a permanent solution to FX settlement risk — a problem that remained unaddressed for nearly three more decades, until CLS Bank was established in 2002.

3.2 What is CLS Bank?

CLS Bank International (Continuous Linked Settlement) was established in 2002 as an industry-owned utility to eliminate FX settlement risk. It is regulated by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York (as a special-purpose bank) and is subject to oversight by a college of central banks representing the currencies it settles. CLS settles 18 major currencies including USD, EUR, GBP, JPY, CHF, CAD, AUD, HKD, SGD, NOK, SEK, DKK, NZD, ZAR, ILS, MXN, HUF, and KRW.

CLS has approximately 75 settlement members (major global banks) and over 25,000 third-party participants who access CLS indirectly through settlement members. On a typical day, CLS settles over $6 trillion in FX trades, representing approximately 50% of global FX market turnover.

Settlement Members vs Third-Party Participants

Settlement Members (~75)

Major global banks (JP Morgan, Citibank, Deutsche Bank, HSBC, BNP Paribas, etc.) that connect directly to CLS Bank. Settlement members hold accounts at CLS in all 18 currencies, submit payment instructions directly to CLS via SWIFT, fund their own net short positions each day, and are responsible for their clients' (third-party participants') obligations to CLS.
Third-Party Participants (25,000+)

Corporates, hedge funds, asset managers, and smaller banks that access CLS indirectly via a settlement member. The settlement member acts as their agent — submitting their trades to CLS, funding their pay-in obligations, and receiving their pay-outs. Third-party participants never interact directly with CLS.

3.3 How CLS Works — Payment vs Payment (PvP)

The core mechanism of CLS is Payment vs Payment (PvP): both legs of an FX trade are settled simultaneously. CLS achieves this by acting as a central settlement bank — holding accounts in all 18 currencies at each respective central bank. Settlement occurs in central bank money, which is the safest form of settlement asset.

The CLS Settlement Process — Step by Step

1

Trade Matching

Two settlement members (or their clients) execute an FX trade. Both parties submit the trade details to CLS (via SWIFT) independently. CLS matches the two submissions — confirming the amount, currency pair, settlement date, and counterparties match.

2

Position Calculation (00:00–06:30 CET)

CLS calculates each member's net funding obligation for the day in each currency. Due to multilateral netting across all matched trades, the net amounts are a small fraction of the gross. Funding efficiency typically reduces gross flows by 96%+ — $6T gross settles with only ~$200–300B in net funding.

3

Pay-In Window (07:00–12:00 CET)

Members must fund their net short positions by paying into CLS's accounts at the respective central banks. Pay-in schedules are staggered across currencies. If a member fails to fund, CLS can reschedule or exclude their trades.

4

Settlement (Continuous)

As funding arrives, CLS settles trades on a PvP basis — simultaneously crediting the buyer and debiting the seller in both currencies at the same moment. No party can receive without simultaneously delivering. The ratchet approach ensures pay-ins always exceed pay-outs throughout the day.

5

Pay-Out (After Settlement)

After all trades are settled, CLS pays out net long positions back to members. The day ends with CLS holding zero balances — all positions flatten out.

Key Insight: At no point does CLS extend credit. Members must fund their short positions before CLS releases any pay-outs. This "ratchet" approach — where pay-ins exceed pay-outs throughout the day — ensures CLS always has sufficient funds to complete settlement.

3.4 CLS Transaction Processing Details

Eligible Products

CLS settles: FX Spot (T+2), FX Forwards (forward value date), FX Swaps (near and far legs), FX Options (premium and exercise), and Non-Deliverable Forwards (NDFs) for certain currency pairs where delivery of the restricted currency is not possible. NDFs are settled in a deliverable currency (typically USD).

SWIFT Messaging in CLS

CLS members submit payment instructions to CLS using SWIFT messages. The primary message type used is MT 304 (Advice/Instruction of a Third Party Deal) for FX confirmation. Settlement instructions flow via SWIFT FIN. In the ISO 20022 migration, CLS has adopted fxtr (FX Trade) message types — fxtr.014 (FX Trade Confirmation) and fxtr.016 (FX Trade Status). Each member connects to CLS via their SWIFT BIC. CLS Bank holds a corresponding account at each of the 18 central banks, creating the infrastructure for simultaneous multi-currency settlement.

Benefits of CLS

✅

Eliminates Principal Risk

PvP means neither party can lose the full principal of an FX trade. The maximum exposure is eliminated entirely — only replacement cost risk remains.

💧

Liquidity Efficiency

Multilateral netting reduces gross settlement flows by ~96%. $6T gross settles with only ~$200–300B in net funding — freeing enormous bank liquidity.

🌐

Central Bank Money

Settlement occurs in central bank money at each respective central bank — the ultimate risk-free settlement asset.

📋

Regulatory Capital Relief

CLS-settled trades attract lower regulatory capital requirements under Basel III due to reduced settlement risk.

3.5 CLS Default Management Waterfall

In the event that a CLS settlement member fails to meet its pay-in obligation, CLS has a structured default management framework to protect remaining participants and ensure the settlement day completes.

1

Trade Rescheduling

CLS's first response to a funding failure is to reschedule the failing member's trades — removing them from the current settlement cycle and resetting them for settlement the following day, or excluding them entirely. This protects the rest of the system without triggering the capital waterfall in most cases.

2

Defaulter's Prefunded Balances

The defaulting member's existing balances held at CLS across all 18 currencies are consumed first. CLS applies these to cover any shortfall from trades that cannot be rescheduled.

3

CLS's Own Capital

CLS Bank maintains its own capital reserves available to absorb losses exceeding the defaulter's available balances. CLS is specifically capitalised to withstand the default of its largest settlement member.

4

Loss Sharing Among Surviving Members

Remaining losses are allocated pro-rata among the surviving settlement members per the pre-agreed loss-sharing arrangement in the CLS rulebook. Each member knows their maximum potential loss-sharing exposure before joining CLS.

3.6 Residual FX Risk Outside CLS

Despite CLS's success, a significant portion of global FX activity still settles outside CLS, leaving residual settlement risk in the market.

🌍

Non-CLS Currencies

Only 18 currencies are eligible. Major emerging market currencies — Brazilian Real (BRL), Indian Rupee (INR), Russian Ruble (RUB), Turkish Lira (TRY) — are not eligible. FX trades in these currencies must settle bilaterally with full Herstatt risk.

🏢

Non-CLS Participants

Many regional banks, corporates, and non-bank financial institutions trade FX but settle bilaterally outside CLS, not being settlement members or third-party participants.

📄

NDF Residual Risk

NDFs are settled in USD via CLS — the payment obligation is covered. However, the underlying currency exposure is not eliminated; only the USD payment settlement risk is addressed.

📉

BIS Estimates

BIS research estimates ~1/3 of daily FX turnover (~$2–2.5 trillion) still settles outside CLS, remaining exposed to Herstatt risk. Expanding CLS coverage is a BIS FSB priority.
